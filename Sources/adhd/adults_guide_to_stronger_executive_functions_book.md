## Introduction: The 7 Executive Function Deficits Tied to ADHD

*By Russell A. Barkley, Ph.D.*

Adults with ADHD have significant difficulty with executive functions — the mental abilities that allow us to plan, organize, and manage our time and behavior. These deficits are not a matter of intelligence or effort. They are neurological in nature, rooted in differences in brain development and function.

To understand executive functions, think of the brain as having two systems: a "doing" system (the limbic regions) and a "managing" system (the prefrontal cortex). In people with ADHD, the managing system is underactive. It does not adequately regulate the doing system. The result is behavior that appears impulsive, disorganized, emotionally driven, and focused on the present at the expense of the future.

Executive functions are the mental abilities that allow us to pursue goals across time. They include the capacity to inhibit behavior, to hold information in working memory, to regulate our emotions and motivation, to plan and problem-solve, and to monitor our own performance. When these abilities are impaired, as they are in ADHD, people struggle in virtually every domain of life.

Here are the seven executive function deficits most closely tied to ADHD:

### 1. Inhibition

Inhibition is the ability to stop yourself from acting on your first impulse. It is the foundation of all other executive functions. Without the ability to pause and reflect, all the other self-management abilities break down. People with ADHD act before thinking, say things they regret, and make impulsive decisions that create problems at home, at work, and in relationships.

### 2. Working Memory

Working memory is the ability to hold information in mind and work with it mentally. It is your mental workspace. People with ADHD often forget what they were about to say or do, lose track of where they are in a task, fail to remember instructions, and have difficulty relating new information to what they already know. There are two types of working memory: verbal (holding words and numbers in mind) and non-verbal (holding images, sequences, and temporal information in mind).

### 3. Emotional Self-Regulation

People with ADHD experience emotions as intensely as everyone else, but they have a reduced ability to manage and modulate those emotions. They are more reactive, less able to calm themselves down, and more likely to display their emotional states openly. This can damage relationships and lead to a reputation for being unstable, explosive, or difficult. Emotional self-regulation involves tolerating frustration, separating feelings from actions, and soothing oneself.

### 4. Self-Motivation

Motivation in neurotypical people is influenced by future consequences. For people with ADHD, future consequences have very little power. What drives behavior is the immediacy of reward or interest — the now. This makes it difficult to work on tasks that are not immediately rewarding, interesting, challenging, or urgent. People with ADHD struggle to persist on boring tasks, to start tasks well in advance of deadlines, and to sustain effort when there is no external source of motivation.

### 5. Planning and Problem-Solving

Planning requires the ability to mentally simulate the future, anticipate obstacles, and devise strategies to reach goals. People with ADHD have difficulty breaking tasks into steps, anticipating what can go wrong, organizing their approach, and monitoring their progress. Problem-solving requires flexibility of thought, and ADHD impairs the ability to shift strategies when one approach is not working.

### 6. Verbal Working Memory (Self-Speech)

Private speech — the internal voice we use to guide our own behavior, remind ourselves of rules, and talk through problems — is delayed in its development in people with ADHD. This delays the internalization of rules and instructions, makes it hard to follow complex verbal directions, and reduces the use of verbal self-instruction as a self-management tool.

### 7. Self-Awareness

The ability to observe ourselves objectively — to step back and evaluate our own behavior, performance, and impact on others — is an executive function that is significantly impaired in ADHD. People with ADHD are often unaware of how they are perceived by others, do not accurately assess their own performance, and have difficulty recognizing when they need to change course. Self-awareness is the foundation of insight, and without it, self-improvement is difficult.

These seven deficits interact and compound one another. A person with poor inhibition will also have difficulty using working memory, regulating emotions, and planning effectively. Treatment for ADHD must address this full profile of executive function deficits, not just attention and hyperactivity.

The chapters that follow address each of these executive function areas in detail, offering research-based strategies and real-world tools for managing them more effectively.

## Chapter 1: The ADHD–Executive Function Link

### What Are Executive Functions?

Executive functions are the brain-based skills required to execute tasks: to begin them, sustain them, shift between them, and complete them. They are the cognitive processes that lie at the heart of goal-directed behavior. Every time you resist a temptation, remember a commitment, juggle competing priorities, or adjust your approach in the face of new information, you are using executive functions.

For people with ADHD, these functions are impaired. Not all of them, and not all the time, but across a wide range of situations and demands, executive function deficits show up as a defining characteristic of the disorder.

The term "executive functions" comes from the metaphor of an executive — a CEO of a company — who plans, delegates, monitors, and adjusts. The brain's executive, the prefrontal cortex, performs the same role for the whole person. It manages attention, regulates emotion, integrates past and present to guide future behavior, and inhibits impulses that interfere with goals.

### The ADHD Brain

ADHD involves a delay in the development and functioning of the prefrontal cortex. Brain imaging studies show that the cortical development in children with ADHD lags behind that of neurotypical children by approximately three years. In some cases, these differences persist into adulthood.

The neurotransmitters dopamine and norepinephrine play a central role. These chemicals are involved in the reward and motivation circuitry of the brain. When they are not working properly, the brain does not respond normally to delayed rewards, future consequences, or the intrinsic motivation required to sustain effort on non-preferred tasks.

This is why ADHD is not simply a problem of attention. It is a problem of self-regulation — of managing one's own behavior across time toward goals, especially when the rewards for doing so are delayed or uncertain.

### ADHD Is Not a Knowledge Problem

One of the most important — and most misunderstood — aspects of ADHD is this: people with ADHD typically know what they should do. They can explain good time management, they understand the consequences of procrastination, and they can describe what organization looks like. Their problem is not knowing. Their problem is doing.

Executive functions are performance systems. They are required at the point of performance — at the moment when behavior needs to be governed by what you know. ADHD disrupts the connection between knowing and doing. This is why advice, motivation, and understanding rarely solve the problem by themselves. What is needed is restructuring the environment so that the right cues, prompts, consequences, and accountability are present at the moment of performance.

### The Six Components of Executive Function (Barkley's Model)

Russell Barkley has proposed a model of ADHD that centers on six executive functions, all of which depend on the foundational skill of behavioral inhibition:

- **Inhibition:** Stopping dominant responses, interrupting ongoing behavior when it is not working, and protecting the space for the other executive functions to operate
- **Non-verbal working memory:** Holding events, images, and sequences in mind to guide behavior across time
- **Verbal working memory:** Using private speech to guide behavior, follow rules, and talk through problems
- **Emotional self-regulation:** Managing emotional arousal and motivation to pursue goals
- **Planning and problem-solving:** Mentally simulating actions before carrying them out, generating options, and selecting among them
- **Reconstitution:** Breaking apart complex behavior into components and recombining them in flexible, novel ways

These six functions work together as a system. Strengthening any one of them tends to support the others.

### The Time Horizon Problem

One of the most practical implications of executive function impairment in ADHD is what might be called the time horizon problem. For most people, future rewards and consequences exert a powerful influence on present behavior. For people with ADHD, that influence is dramatically reduced. The future is, in an important neurological sense, less real.

This is why people with ADHD are not simply lazy. Laziness implies not caring about consequences. People with ADHD often care deeply — they want to do well, they want to achieve their goals, they feel genuine distress about their failures. But the neurological machinery that normally converts caring into doing, especially when the payoff is distant, does not work the way it should.

The practical implication is that strategies need to make consequences more immediate. External deadlines, accountability partners, rewards for interim steps, and time limits all serve to bring the future closer.

### Emotions and ADHD

Emotional dysregulation is now recognized as a core feature of ADHD, even though it is not included in the formal diagnostic criteria. Research shows that the vast majority of adults with ADHD have significant difficulties regulating their emotions — managing frustration, controlling anger, tolerating boredom, and recovering from rejection.

The concept of **rejection sensitive dysphoria (RSD)** — an intense emotional response to perceived criticism, failure, or rejection — has emerged as one of the most clinically important aspects of ADHD in adults. RSD can be more disabling than the attention symptoms themselves. It affects career choices, relationship patterns, and self-esteem.

### Self-Esteem and the ADHD Narrative

Adults with ADHD have typically accumulated decades of failure, criticism, and self-doubt by the time they receive a diagnosis. They have been told they are not trying hard enough, that they are irresponsible, that they are smart but not living up to their potential. These messages leave scars.

A diagnosis of ADHD, when properly understood, can be a turning point. It reframes a lifetime of struggles not as character flaws but as symptoms of a neurological condition. This shift — from "I am broken" to "I have a brain that works differently" — is not merely comforting. It is the foundation for the self-compassion and self-understanding that effective management of ADHD requires.

### Strategies That Work

Because ADHD is a performance problem, the most effective strategies are those that change the environment in ways that support performance:

- **Externalize time:** Use timers, clocks, calendars, and alarms to make the passage of time visible
- **Externalize reminders:** Use written lists, visual cues, and physical prompts rather than relying on memory
- **Externalize motivation:** Build in accountability, rewards, and deadlines that make consequences more immediate
- **Break tasks down:** Reduce the distance between the present and the goal by creating smaller steps with their own mini-deadlines
- **Manage transitions:** Use planned routines to reduce the friction of starting and stopping tasks
- **Reduce distractions:** Create environments that minimize the demands on self-control

The following chapters address each of the major executive function domains in detail, providing strategies grounded in research and tested in real life.

## Chapter 2: Emotional Self-Regulation: Strategies for Managing Big Feelings

### Emotions and ADHD: More Than Meets the Eye

For decades, ADHD was defined primarily as a disorder of attention and hyperactivity. Emotional dysregulation — the difficulty managing the intensity, duration, and expression of emotions — was either overlooked or dismissed as secondary. Research has since made clear that emotion dysregulation is not secondary at all. For many adults with ADHD, it is the most disabling aspect of the condition.

People with ADHD experience the same emotions as everyone else. The difference is in regulation: the ability to modulate emotional responses, to calm down after being upset, to prevent emotional reactions from hijacking behavior, and to recover from setbacks with resilience rather than spiraling into despair.

The reasons for this difficulty are neurological. The prefrontal cortex — the brain region most affected by ADHD — plays a central role in regulating emotion. When this region is underactive, emotional responses from the limbic system are less effectively modulated, and the result is emotional reactivity that feels out of proportion to the situation.

### Deficient Emotional Self-Regulation (DESR)

**Deficient emotional self-regulation (DESR)** refers to the pattern of emotional difficulties seen in ADHD: low frustration tolerance, irritability, hot temper, emotional excitability, and difficulty inhibiting emotional responses. Research by Russell Barkley and others has suggested that DESR should be considered a formal component of ADHD, given how consistently it appears and how significantly it contributes to impairment.

DESR is not the same as a mood disorder, though the two can co-occur. In DESR, emotions tend to be intense and quick to arise, but they are also relatively short-lived. The person does not sustain a depressed or manic episode; they have an explosive reaction that subsides and passes. In the aftermath, many people with ADHD feel genuine remorse and confusion about why they reacted so intensely.

### Rejection Sensitive Dysphoria

**Rejection sensitive dysphoria (RSD)** is the term coined by William Dodson, M.D., to describe the extreme emotional sensitivity to perceived rejection, criticism, failure, or teasing that is common in people with ADHD. The word "dysphoria" comes from the Greek meaning "difficult to bear," and that is exactly how people with RSD describe it: an overwhelming, almost physical pain triggered by the perception that they have failed, disappointed someone, or been rejected.

RSD is not simply being sensitive. The emotional response is instant — it does not build gradually — and it is extreme. People with RSD describe feeling like the world has ended, experiencing waves of shame, grief, or rage that come on in seconds and can last for hours. They may burst into tears, withdraw completely, lash out in anger, or become paralyzed.

Because RSD is triggered by the perception of rejection or failure — not necessarily the reality of it — it can be set off by a neutral look from a colleague, a brief reply to a text, or a comment that was not intended critically at all. This makes it highly unpredictable and profoundly exhausting.

**The impact of RSD on daily life:**

- Avoiding situations where criticism or failure is possible
- Underachieving to avoid the risk of trying and failing
- People-pleasing and conflict avoidance at all costs
- Overreacting to minor slights in ways that damage relationships
- Chronic low-grade shame and a negative self-image
- Misdiagnosis as borderline personality disorder or bipolar disorder

**Treatment options for RSD:**

- **Alpha-2 agonists** such as guanfacine (Intuniv) and clonidine, which modulate the norepinephrine system and can reduce the intensity of the emotional response
- **MAOIs** (monoamine oxidase inhibitors) such as phenelzine, which have been shown to reduce RSD in some patients
- **Stimulant medications** that treat ADHD may also reduce RSD by improving prefrontal regulation
- **Cognitive behavioral therapy (CBT)** to address the thinking patterns that amplify and sustain RSD reactions
- **Dialectical behavior therapy (DBT)** skills, particularly distress tolerance and emotion regulation modules

### Managing Anger with ADHD

Anger is one of the most common emotional difficulties for adults with ADHD. The same impulsivity that leads to blurting out comments or making hasty decisions also leads to outbursts of anger — intense, fast, and often regretted immediately afterward.

For some people with ADHD, anger comes on suddenly and overwhelmingly, with little sense of a gradual buildup. The frustration, annoyance, or hurt that a neurotypical person might notice and manage over time hits all at once, like a switch being flipped. This can make anger management strategies that rely on recognizing early warning signs difficult to implement.

**Strategies for managing anger:**

- **Create physical distance** when you feel anger rising. Leaving the room before speaking is not avoidance; it is a legitimate strategy to prevent damage.
- **Delay your response.** Tell the other person: "I need some time. I'll come back to this in an hour." Honor that commitment.
- **Exercise as a discharge valve.** Physical exercise metabolizes the physiological arousal associated with anger and shortens its duration.
- **Identify your triggers.** Keep a log of situations that reliably produce anger. Awareness of patterns is the first step to intervention.
- **Prepare responses in advance.** For recurring situations that reliably produce anger, script your response ahead of time so you have something ready to say rather than reacting in the moment.
- **Work with a therapist** trained in ADHD and anger management. CBT-based approaches that target the thought patterns fueling anger can be highly effective.
- **Medication review.** If anger is significantly impairing your life, discuss it explicitly with your prescriber. Some medications are more helpful for emotional dysregulation than others.

### Self-Test: Do You Struggle with Emotional Regulation?

The following questions can help you assess whether emotional self-regulation is a significant challenge for you:

- Do you often feel emotions more intensely than the situation seems to call for?
- Do you have difficulty calming yourself down after becoming upset?
- Do you feel devastated by criticism, even when it is mild or constructive?
- Do you avoid situations where you might fail or be criticized, even when the opportunity is worthwhile?
- Do you often regret things you said or did when you were emotionally activated?
- Have your emotional reactions damaged important relationships?
- Do you experience sudden waves of intense emotion that seem to come out of nowhere?
- Do people describe you as "too sensitive" or "emotional"?

If you answered yes to several of these questions, emotional self-regulation is likely a meaningful challenge for you. This is a recognized aspect of ADHD, not a character flaw, and it responds to appropriate treatment.

### Cognitive Behavioral Therapy for Emotional Dysregulation

CBT offers a structured approach to understanding and managing emotional dysregulation. The core insight of CBT is that thoughts, feelings, and behaviors are connected — and that by changing how we think about situations, we can change how we feel and behave.

For adults with ADHD and emotional dysregulation, CBT work typically focuses on:

- **Identifying automatic thoughts** that intensify emotional reactions (e.g., "Everyone thinks I'm stupid," "This always happens to me," "I've ruined everything")
- **Challenging cognitive distortions** such as catastrophizing, mind-reading, and all-or-nothing thinking
- **Building frustration tolerance** through graduated exposure to situations that typically provoke intense emotions
- **Developing coping statements** that can interrupt the escalation of emotional reactions
- **Processing past experiences** that have shaped current emotional patterns, particularly the accumulated pain of a lifetime of ADHD-related failures and criticism

CBT for ADHD and emotional dysregulation is typically more structured and active than insight-oriented therapy. It involves homework, skill practice, and concrete techniques rather than open-ended exploration.

### DBT Skills for Emotional Regulation

Dialectical behavior therapy (DBT), originally developed for borderline personality disorder, has become widely used for emotional dysregulation in ADHD. The DBT skills that are most relevant include:

**Distress tolerance skills** — techniques for getting through intense emotional moments without making things worse:
- **TIPP:** Temperature (cold water on the face to activate the dive reflex), Intense exercise, Paced breathing, Progressive muscle relaxation
- **ACCEPTS:** Activities, Contributing, Comparisons, Emotions (opposite), Pushing away, Thoughts (other), Sensations
- **Self-soothe** using the five senses

**Emotion regulation skills** — techniques for reducing the intensity and duration of emotional reactions:
- Identifying and labeling emotions
- Checking the facts (is your interpretation of the situation accurate?)
- Opposite action (acting opposite to the urge the emotion creates)
- Building positive experiences to create a buffer against emotional reactivity

**Mindfulness skills** — observing emotions without being driven by them:
- Noticing emotions without judging them
- Riding out emotional waves without acting on them
- Returning to the present moment

### Reader Perspectives: Living with Emotional Dysregulation

*"The hardest part is not the anger itself — it's what comes after. The shame. Knowing I hurt someone I love because I couldn't stop myself in time. I've gotten better with therapy, but it's still work every day."*

*"RSD almost ended my career. I was so afraid of being criticized that I stopped volunteering for anything. My therapist helped me see that I was managing the fear by shrinking my life."*

*"I didn't know until my 40s that what I experienced as 'sensitivity' was actually a neurological symptom. That reframe changed everything. I stopped blaming myself for it."*

### Expert Q&A: Understanding and Treating RSD

**Q: Is RSD unique to ADHD?**

A: RSD appears to be largely — though perhaps not exclusively — an ADHD phenomenon. It is not listed in the DSM diagnostic criteria for ADHD, which is part of why it goes unrecognized and untreated so often. But clinically, it is one of the most consistent features reported by adults with ADHD. The neurological mechanisms are consistent with ADHD: the same dopamine and norepinephrine systems that regulate attention and impulsivity also regulate the emotional pain response.

**Q: What's the most effective treatment for RSD?**

A: For many patients, alpha-2 agonists like guanfacine are remarkably effective at reducing the intensity of the RSD response. They do not prevent all emotional pain, but they seem to take the edge off the most extreme reactions. CBT and DBT can also be effective, particularly for the thinking patterns and avoidance behaviors that RSD generates. Stimulant medications help some people but not all. The most effective approach is typically a combination of medication and therapy.

**Q: What can family members do to help?**

A: The most important thing is to understand that RSD is not manipulation or immaturity. It is a genuine neurological response that the person with ADHD does not choose and often cannot simply talk themselves out of. Avoiding unnecessary criticism, delivering feedback in a calm and non-judgmental way, and understanding that overreactions are not about you personally are all helpful. Encouraging the person to seek treatment is important, because RSD is treatable.

## Chapter 3: Motivation: Overcoming Procrastination and Getting Things Done

### The ADHD Motivation Problem

Motivation is not simply a matter of wanting something badly enough. For people with ADHD, motivation is neurologically impaired in a very specific way: the brain's response to future rewards is blunted. The normal mechanism by which anticipated future outcomes influence present behavior is unreliable.

This is why people with ADHD can be highly motivated to do things they find interesting, novel, urgent, or immediately rewarding — and essentially unable to begin or sustain tasks that are boring, routine, or rewarding only in the distant future. It is not hypocrisy or inconsistency. It is the signature of a brain that is driven by the present, not the future.

Understanding this helps explain many of the most frustrating patterns associated with ADHD:

- Starting projects with tremendous enthusiasm and abandoning them when the novelty fades
- Meeting external deadlines but never the self-imposed ones
- Doing things urgently only when the deadline is imminent
- Being "lazy" with things that are important but not interesting
- Working brilliantly in a crisis but struggling in routine conditions

### Procrastination and ADHD

Procrastination is universal, but in ADHD it is particularly severe and pervasive. For adults with ADHD, procrastination is rarely a simple matter of prioritization or time management. It involves the interplay of multiple executive function deficits:

- **Working memory deficits** mean that tasks that are out of sight are genuinely out of mind
- **Time blindness** means that deadlines feel distant until they are suddenly immediate
- **Initiation problems** mean that starting is genuinely harder than continuing
- **Emotional avoidance** means that tasks associated with past failure, anxiety, or shame are particularly hard to begin
- **Perfectionism** means that fear of doing something imperfectly prevents starting at all

The experience of ADHD procrastination is often described not as laziness but as paralysis: knowing you need to do something, wanting to do it, and being unable to begin. This is why motivation and willpower approaches — "just do it," "think about how good you'll feel when it's done" — are so ineffective. They address the wrong problem.

### CBT for Procrastination

Cognitive behavioral therapy for procrastination targets both the thinking patterns and the behavioral patterns that maintain it.

**Common thinking patterns that fuel procrastination:**

- *"I work better under pressure"* (a justification for delay that rarely reflects reality)
- *"I need to be in the right mood to start"* (waiting for motivation that is unlikely to arrive spontaneously)
- *"If I can't do it perfectly, there's no point starting"* (perfectionism disguised as standards)
- *"There's still plenty of time"* (underestimating how long tasks will take)
- *"It's not that important"* (minimizing to reduce discomfort)

**CBT techniques for overcoming procrastination:**

- **Behavioral experiments:** Test your assumptions. Do you actually work better under pressure, or does your work simply get submitted under pressure regardless of quality?
- **Activity scheduling:** Schedule specific times for specific tasks, treating them like appointments you cannot cancel
- **Graded task assignment:** Break the avoided task into the smallest possible starting step and commit only to that step
- **Self-compassion practice:** Reduce the shame and self-criticism that make avoidance feel emotionally necessary
- **Thought records:** Identify and challenge the automatic thoughts that arise when you consider starting an avoided task

### Procrastivity: The Productive Procrastinator

One of the most recognizable patterns in ADHD is **procrastivity** — the tendency to be extremely productive at everything except the one thing you most need to do. The house gets cleaned. Emails get sorted. A minor errand gets completed. Everything except the important task.

Procrastivity is not random. It serves a function: it allows the person to feel productive (reducing some of the guilt and anxiety associated with avoidance) while still not confronting the avoided task. Understanding it as a coping mechanism — ineffective, but motivated — makes it more amenable to change.

**Strategies for interrupting procrastivity:**

- Make the avoided task the first task of the day, before any other work is permitted
- Remove the option of alternative productive activities (close email, put away other projects)
- Use a timer to create a fixed, bounded period of engagement with the avoided task
- Ask yourself: "Why is this task the one I'm avoiding?" — the answer often reveals an underlying fear or assumption that can be addressed

### Motivation Hacks for the ADHD Brain

Because the ADHD brain is driven by interest, novelty, challenge, urgency, and passion, strategies that harness these drives are more effective than those that rely on self-discipline or future consequences.

**Strategies for making tasks more motivating:**

- **Add novelty:** Change your environment, use new tools, listen to new music, work in a different location
- **Add urgency:** Set a timer, create a fake deadline, commit to a specific end time, tell someone you'll have it done by a certain hour
- **Add challenge:** Turn the task into a game, compete with yourself, track your performance, look for ways to do the task better or faster
- **Add interest:** Find the inherently interesting aspect of the task, connect it to something you care about, learn something new in the process of doing it
- **Add a reward:** Plan an immediate reward for completion, make it something you genuinely enjoy, deliver it reliably
- **Add accountability:** Tell someone what you're going to do and when, work alongside another person (body doubling), report on your progress

### The Twelve-Step Motivation Program for ADHD Adults

Drawing on research and clinical experience, here is a comprehensive approach to rebuilding motivation in adults with ADHD:

1. **Understand the neurological basis of your motivation difficulties.** You are not lazy. Your brain genuinely does not respond normally to distant rewards. This is not a character problem; it is a wiring problem.

2. **Stop waiting to feel motivated before starting.** Motivation follows action in ADHD, not the other way around. Starting, however small the start, generates the dopamine that sustains engagement.

3. **Identify your optimal conditions for working.** Do you work better with noise or silence? In the morning or evening? In short bursts or long sessions? With other people or alone? Design your work environment to match your actual preferences, not idealized ones.

4. **Create an external accountability structure.** Working with an ADHD coach, an accountability partner, or a study group provides the external scaffolding that your internal motivation system cannot reliably supply.

5. **Use the two-minute rule.** If a task can be done in two minutes, do it now. For tasks that cannot be done in two minutes, begin with a two-minute start: commit to doing just two minutes of work on the task. Most of the time, once started, you will continue.

6. **Manage your energy, not just your time.** Schedule your most demanding cognitive work for when your energy and focus are naturally highest. Do not try to do creative or analytical work when you are mentally depleted.

7. **Build in recovery time.** People with ADHD often either hyperfocus until exhaustion or struggle to begin because they are already depleted. Scheduled breaks, physical exercise, and adequate sleep are not luxuries — they are prerequisites for cognitive performance.

8. **Address perfectionism directly.** If perfectionism is a significant driver of procrastination, work with a therapist on the beliefs and fears that underlie it. "Good enough" is not a failure; it is a necessary adaptation for high-functioning adults.

9. **Reduce friction in your environment.** Make the things you need to do as easy as possible to begin. Lay out your exercise clothes the night before. Keep important items in designated, visible places. Prepare workspaces in advance.

10. **Celebrate completion, not just quality.** Acknowledge when you finish things, regardless of how well. Completion is genuinely difficult for ADHD brains, and it deserves recognition.

11. **Revisit your values and goals regularly.** When day-to-day motivation is difficult, connecting tasks to larger meaning and purpose can provide a longer-horizon source of motivation. What matters to you? How does this task, however unpleasant, serve that?

12. **Get treatment if you haven't.** Medication, therapy, and coaching for ADHD all have meaningful evidence for improving motivation and reducing procrastination. If you are not in treatment, or if your current treatment is not addressing motivation difficulties, discuss this with your provider.

### Self-Test: Is Procrastination a Problem for You?

- Do you routinely start important tasks only when the deadline is immediate?
- Do you often feel paralyzed when faced with beginning a task, even one you want to do?
- Do you spend time doing other tasks to avoid the one you most need to do?
- Do you underestimate how long tasks will take, leading to chronic lateness or incompleteness?
- Do you tell yourself you work better under pressure, even though the evidence is mixed?
- Do you feel disproportionate anxiety or dread when you think about starting certain tasks?
- Have missed deadlines or incomplete projects created significant problems in your life?

### Reader Perspectives: Procrastination and ADHD

*"I've missed so many deadlines not because I didn't care but because I genuinely couldn't make myself start. I'd sit at my desk for hours, knowing I needed to work, not doing it. It's not laziness. It's agony."*

*"The two-minute rule changed my life. I don't try to do the whole thing. I just do two minutes. Nine times out of ten, I do more than two minutes. But even when I don't, I've started — and that's everything."*

*"Body doubling is the single most effective strategy I've found. I rent a coworking space specifically so I'm around other people who are working. Something about it makes my brain turn on."*

## Chapter 4: Prioritization and Planning: Tackling Indecision, Time Blindness, and To-Do Lists

### Time Blindness

**Time blindness** is one of the most disabling — and least understood — aspects of ADHD. It refers to the impaired sense of time experienced by people with ADHD: difficulty perceiving the passage of time, estimating how long tasks will take, feeling the urgency of future deadlines, and relating the present moment to the past and future.

For neurotypical people, time exists as a continuous ribbon stretching from the past through the present into the future. They can feel how much time has passed and anticipate how much remains. For people with ADHD, there are often only two times: now and not now. The future is not-now, which means it has little psychological reality until it suddenly becomes now.

This explains why people with ADHD:
- Are chronically late, not because they disrespect others' time but because they misjudge how much time tasks will take
- Feel blindsided by deadlines that have been on the calendar for weeks
- Hyperfocus for hours without awareness that time is passing
- Wait until the last possible moment to begin tasks, not from laziness but because the deadline doesn't feel real until it is imminent

**Strategies for managing time blindness:**

- **Use analog clocks.** Analog clocks provide a visual representation of time passing — the sweeping hand, the shrinking arc before the hour. Digital clocks show a number; analog clocks show time. Many people with ADHD find analog clocks significantly more useful.
- **Set multiple alarms.** Not just one alarm for the time you need to leave, but a series: 60 minutes before, 30 minutes, 15 minutes, and "leave now."
- **Use a visual timer.** Timers like the Time Timer, which show a red disk shrinking as time passes, make the passage of time concrete and visible.
- **Estimate, then double.** When estimating how long a task will take, deliberately double your estimate. People with ADHD consistently underestimate time.
- **Time yourself on routine tasks.** Discover how long getting ready in the morning actually takes, how long the drive to work actually is. Use this data rather than your (unreliable) intuition.
- **Build in transition time.** ADHD brains resist transitions. Allow explicit time for ending one activity and preparing for the next.

### Temporal Discounting and ADHD

**Temporal discounting** refers to the psychological phenomenon by which we value near-term rewards more highly than equally large rewards that are farther in the future. Everyone does this to some degree — $100 today feels worth more than $100 in a year. But in ADHD, temporal discounting is extreme: the future is dramatically discounted compared to the present.

This has profound implications for behavior. When the present reward (watching TV, scrolling social media, staying in bed) is concrete and immediate, and the future reward (completing the report, exercising, getting to work on time) is abstract and delayed, the brain's motivation system reliably favors the present. This is not a choice or a value; it is a neurological pattern.

**Strategies for counteracting temporal discounting:**

- **Bring rewards forward.** Make immediate rewards contingent on completing tasks. Plan something enjoyable immediately after a hard task.
- **Make future consequences more vivid.** Visualize the specific negative consequences of delay — not vaguely, but in detail. What will your boss say? How will you feel the night before the deadline?
- **Use commitment devices.** Bet money, tell people publicly, arrange for consequences that make following through more rewarding than not.
- **Build long-term habits through short-term incentives.** Do not rely on the distant benefits of a habit to sustain it. Find immediate pleasures that can be paired with the habit.

### Doomscrolling and Attention Capture

The modern attention economy is specifically designed to capture and hold attention using the same mechanisms that ADHD hijacks. Notifications, infinite scroll, variable reward schedules (like social media feeds), and algorithmically personalized content are extraordinarily effective at capturing the ADHD brain and making time vanish.

**Strategies for managing digital distraction:**

- **Delete social media apps from your phone** and access them only from a desktop computer
- **Use app blockers** like Freedom or Cold Turkey to make distracting sites unavailable during work time
- **Turn off all non-essential notifications.** The cost of a notification interruption is not just the time spent on it but the recovery time — the time to return to full focus on the interrupted task.
- **Set designated scrolling times** rather than permitting access at any time
- **Use grayscale mode** on your phone; the reduced visual stimulation makes screens less compelling

### Analog Clocks and the ADHD Brain

Research and clinical experience have both suggested that analog clocks are more helpful for people with ADHD than digital ones. The reason is visual and spatial: an analog clock makes the relationship between time and space concrete. You can see where 3:00 is relative to 4:00. You can see how much of the hour remains. The ticking provides an auditory cue that time is passing.

For people with time blindness, having a large analog clock visible in every room they regularly occupy can be a simple, low-tech, highly effective intervention.

### Decision-Making with ADHD

Decision-making is impaired in ADHD for several reasons:

- **Working memory limitations** mean that all the relevant options and their consequences cannot be held in mind simultaneously
- **Impulsivity** leads to decisions made on the basis of the first option that feels appealing
- **Time blindness** means the future consequences of decisions are underweighted
- **Emotional dysregulation** means that decisions are often heavily influenced by the current emotional state
- **Analysis paralysis** — the flip side of impulsivity — occurs when awareness of ADHD impulsivity leads to overcorrecting, leading to a state of endless deliberation

**Strategies for better decision-making:**

- **Write it down.** Putting options on paper externalizes working memory and makes comparison possible.
- **Use a simple framework.** For important decisions, identify the two or three most important criteria and evaluate options against those criteria explicitly.
- **Set a decision deadline.** Decide that you will make the decision by a specific time. Open-ended deliberation tends to grow indefinitely.
- **Sleep on it.** For significant decisions, delay the final decision by at least 24 hours. Impulsive decisions made in the heat of the moment are often regretted.
- **Consult a trusted person.** Having a sounding board — a friend, a coach, a therapist — who knows you well can provide perspective and interrupt both impulsive and paralyzed decision patterns.
- **Accept that good enough is enough.** Searching for the perfect option is often what produces paralysis. In most decisions, a good option executed is better than a perfect option perpetually deferred.

### To-Do Lists That Actually Work

Standard to-do lists fail for people with ADHD because:

- They grow without bound
- They don't communicate urgency or priority
- Items that are not immediately relevant disappear from awareness
- The sense of incompleteness creates anxiety rather than motivation

**Principles for ADHD-friendly to-do lists:**

- **Use a daily short list** of three to five items maximum, rather than a comprehensive master list
- **Include only tasks that will actually be done today.** If it won't be done today, it doesn't go on today's list.
- **Write tasks as specific actions,** not vague projects (not "work on report" but "write introduction section of Q3 report")
- **Identify the single most important task** of the day and protect time for it
- **Use a separate capture system** for everything else, so that important items are recorded but don't clutter the daily list
- **Review and rebuild the list** each day rather than carrying over yesterday's incomplete items indefinitely

## Chapter 5: Verbal and Non-Verbal Working Memory: Curbing ADHD Forgetfulness

### The GPS Metaphor for Working Memory

Working memory is sometimes described as the brain's RAM — the short-term workspace where information is held and manipulated. But a more intuitive metaphor may be GPS navigation. When you navigate a familiar route, you are using long-term memory. When you navigate to a new place with GPS, you are relying on real-time guidance — you are following turn-by-turn instructions without internalizing the whole route.

People with ADHD depend heavily on their GPS — on external cues and prompts — because their internal working memory is unreliable. They forget the instructions they just received. They lose their place in multi-step tasks. They remember the errand they needed to do five minutes after they passed the store. Without the GPS, they get lost.

Understanding working memory this way suggests the primary strategy: externalize everything. Don't rely on your internal working memory when external supports can do the same job more reliably.

### Types of Working Memory

**Verbal working memory** is the ability to hold and manipulate words, numbers, and verbal information in mind. It is what you use when you:
- Follow spoken instructions
- Remember a phone number while dialing
- Hold your place in a conversation
- Repeat back what someone just said
- Keep track of steps in a verbal procedure

**Non-verbal working memory** is the ability to hold and manipulate images, sequences, patterns, and information that is spatial or temporal rather than verbal. It is what you use when you:
- Navigate to a place you have been before
- Recall what your schedule looks like today
- Follow a recipe from memory
- Perform physical skills that require sequencing
- Hold a visual image in mind while working

Both types of working memory are impaired in ADHD, though the profile varies from person to person.

### How Working Memory Impairment Shows Up Daily

Working memory deficits in ADHD manifest in countless daily situations:

- Walking into a room and forgetting why
- Forgetting what someone just said in a conversation
- Losing items (keys, phone, wallet) because they were put down without being registered
- Missing appointments despite knowing about them
- Forgetting important events — anniversaries, birthdays, meetings
- Starting tasks and forgetting what the goal was midway through
- Reading a paragraph and having no recollection of what it said
- Interrupting conversations because you need to say something before you forget it
- Making promises you genuinely intended to keep and forgetting them

These are not examples of not caring. They are examples of a failing working memory system.

### Strategies for Verbal Working Memory

- **Write it down immediately.** Never rely on memory for any information that needs to be retained. Carry a small notebook or use a notes app.
- **Repeat back and confirm.** When given verbal instructions, repeat them back aloud. This serves double duty: it confirms accuracy and creates an additional encoding of the information.
- **Use recorded voice memos.** When you have a thought you need to remember, record it immediately. Many people with ADHD find voice memos more effective than text notes because they are faster to create.
- **Set reminders for everything.** Don't decide to remember — set an alarm. Phone reminders, calendar alerts, and smart speaker reminders are not crutches; they are essential tools.
- **Don't trust your memory for conversations.** After important conversations, send a brief follow-up email summarizing what was discussed and agreed. This externalizes the working memory and creates a record.

### Strategies for Non-Verbal Working Memory

- **Use visual schedules and calendars.** Put your schedule where you will see it. A whiteboard in the kitchen, a visible calendar on the wall, a screen that shows today's appointments — these externalize non-verbal working memory.
- **Designated places for everything.** The most common cause of lost items is not having a designated place. Choose specific, consistent locations for high-value items: keys always go on the hook by the door, phone always goes on the charging pad, wallet always goes in the left pocket.
- **Use photos.** Take a photo of where you parked, where you left something, what you were working on. Visual memory is more reliable than verbal memory for many people with ADHD.
- **Checklists for routines.** Morning routines, packing routines, task sequences — reduce them to explicit checklists that do not require working memory.
- **Label and organize storage.** If you can't see it, you'll forget it. Transparent containers, labelled shelves, and organized spaces reduce the working memory demands of daily life.

### The ADHD Memory Self-Test

- Do you frequently forget where you put common items like keys, phone, or wallet?
- Do you forget appointments or commitments despite knowing about them?
- Do you walk into rooms and forget why you went there?
- Do you frequently lose track of what you were saying in the middle of a sentence?
- Do you read material and immediately forget what you read?
- Do you forget to pass along messages to family members or colleagues?
- Do you forget people's names shortly after being introduced?
- Do you forget conversations you had recently?

### Reader Stories: Living with Working Memory Deficits

*"The worst is forgetting things I promised my kids. I genuinely intend to do what I say. I care about my kids more than anything. But the next day, I've forgotten the promise completely. The look on their faces is devastating."*

*"I've learned to treat my phone like an external brain. Every single thing that needs to happen goes into it immediately. I've stopped trying to remember anything. My phone remembers for me. I just have to be disciplined about entering things."*

*"I started taking photos of everything. Where I parked. What I was working on. The instruction manual that I set down somewhere. It sounds excessive but it's changed my life."*

## Chapter 6: Inhibition and Self-Restraint: Managing Impulsivity and Distractions

### Impulsivity in Adults with ADHD

Impulsivity is one of the defining features of ADHD, but in adults it is often misunderstood. It is not primarily about physical restlessness, though that may persist. It is about the brain's difficulty inhibiting the first, most automatic response to a situation.

In the brain, inhibition refers to the neural process by which the prefrontal cortex dampens automatic responses generated by subcortical regions. When this process is impaired — as it is in ADHD — the automatic response tends to win. The person says what they are thinking without filtering it first. They act on the first impulse rather than considering the options. They make decisions based on how things feel right now rather than what makes sense in context.

Adult impulsivity in ADHD shows up in many forms:
- Interrupting and talking over others in conversation
- Making purchases that were not planned and are not affordable
- Starting new projects before completing existing ones
- Quitting jobs, ending relationships, or making major life changes on impulse
- Risk-taking behavior (dangerous driving, sexual impulsivity, substance use)
- Sending emails or texts that were not fully thought through
- Making commitments without checking the calendar or considering feasibility
- Blurting out things that damage relationships

### Impulse Buying and ADHD

Impulse buying is particularly common in ADHD and can create serious financial problems. The same neurological features that make ADHD brains seek novelty and immediate reward make shopping — particularly online shopping with its instant gratification and one-click purchasing — highly activating and difficult to resist.

**Contributing factors:**
- Low frustration tolerance leads to retail therapy
- Novelty-seeking makes new purchases feel exciting
- Difficulty imagining future consequences makes the cost feel abstract
- Emotional dysregulation leads to shopping as a mood management strategy
- Hyperfocusing on a new interest leads to over-investing in it

**Strategies for managing impulse buying:**

- **The 24-hour rule:** For any non-essential purchase over a certain amount (say, $25 or $50), require yourself to wait 24 hours before buying. Most impulse purchases feel much less compelling the next day.
- **Remove friction from saving and add friction to spending:** Move money to savings automatically on payday; remove saved payment information from shopping sites; unsubscribe from promotional emails.
- **The shopping list rule:** Commit to buying only what is on a written list when entering a store or website.
- **Unlink shopping apps** from payment methods; require manual entry of payment information.
- **Identify the emotional trigger:** Keep a brief log of purchases. What were you feeling when you made the purchase? What function was the purchase serving? Understanding the pattern is the first step to interrupting it.
- **Work with a financial coach or therapist** if impulse spending is creating significant problems.

### Budgeting for the ADHD Brain

Standard budgeting approaches — spreadsheets, complex category tracking, envelope systems — often fail for people with ADHD because they require sustained attention, detailed record-keeping, and working memory demands that ADHD interferes with.

**ADHD-friendly budgeting principles:**

- **Automate everything possible.** Automatic bill pay, automatic savings transfers, automatic investments. The fewer financial decisions that require active attention, the better.
- **Use a simple system, not a perfect one.** A rough system that you actually use is infinitely more effective than a precise system that gets abandoned.
- **Separate accounts for separate purposes.** Rather than tracking what you spend in each category, allocate money to separate accounts for different purposes (fixed expenses, variable expenses, savings, discretionary spending). When an account is empty, spending in that category stops.
- **Use visual tools.** Some people with ADHD track spending more effectively with visual representations — graphs, colored charts — than with numbers.
- **Review finances at a scheduled, regular time.** Not whenever you think of it, but at a set weekly time. Treat it as an appointment.
- **Work with a fee-only financial advisor** who understands ADHD if financial management is consistently problematic.

### Social Impulsivity: Oversharing and Slip-Ups

Social impulsivity — saying things in the moment without filtering, oversharing personal information, making inappropriate comments, interrupting — is one of the most relationship-damaging aspects of ADHD impulsivity. It is also one of the most painful, because the consequences are often immediate and visible in the other person's reaction.

People with ADHD often describe knowing, almost immediately after speaking, that they should not have said what they just said. The impulse wins faster than the judgment can intervene. The knowledge comes too late.

**Strategies for managing social impulsivity:**

- **The pause practice:** Before responding in a conversation, practice a two-second pause. It sounds trivial, but even a brief pause can interrupt the automatic response and allow a moment for judgment.
- **Pre-decide filter rules:** Establish personal rules for categories of things you will not share or say in certain contexts (e.g., "I don't share political opinions at work," "I don't tell personal stories in the first meeting").
- **Interrupt scripts:** Develop phrases for interrupting yourself when you notice you have started saying something you should not: "Actually, let me come back to that," "Strike that," "I'm going to rephrase."
- **Prepare for triggering situations:** If certain social contexts reliably produce impulsive speech, prepare for them mentally in advance. Remind yourself of the filter rules you want to apply.
- **Address it directly:** If you say something impulsive that damages a relationship, address it directly and promptly. A genuine, specific apology is more effective than hoping it will be forgotten.

### Fidgeting and Focus

Fidgeting — touching, tapping, moving, fiddling with objects — is often dismissed as a symptom to be eliminated. But research has shown something more nuanced: for people with ADHD, fidgeting may actually support cognitive performance rather than impair it.

The relationship between movement and attention in ADHD appears to be real: motor activity may engage areas of the brain that support focus and working memory. Telling someone with ADHD to "sit still" may actually impair their performance.

**ADHD-friendly approaches to movement and focus:**

- **Fidget tools:** Fidget rings, stress balls, textured pads, spinners — discreet tools that provide motor input without visual distraction
- **Standing desks and movement breaks:** Permitting regular movement rather than requiring sustained stillness
- **Walking meetings:** For phone calls and one-on-one conversations, walking may maintain focus better than sitting
- **Background movement:** Rocking chairs, balance boards, and exercise bikes used while working or studying
- **Exercise as pre-work preparation:** A brief bout of aerobic exercise before cognitively demanding tasks improves executive function and attention

### Managing Distractions

Distractibility in ADHD is not simply being tempted by interesting things. It involves a lower threshold for orienting to any salient stimulus in the environment — sounds, movements, thoughts, sensations — and a reduced ability to disengage from these distractions and return to the primary task.

**Environmental strategies:**

- **Designate a workspace** that is consistently used for work, separate from leisure spaces
- **Use noise-canceling headphones** or white noise to reduce auditory distraction
- **Reduce visual clutter** in the workspace; visual input competes for attention
- **Work in public places** where social norms support quiet, focused behavior
- **Close unnecessary browser tabs and applications** — visual clutter in digital environments is also distracting
- **Communicate your focus needs** to people who share your space

**Digital distraction strategies:**

- Use browser extensions like Freedom, Cold Turkey, or StayFocusd to block distracting sites during work periods
- Enable focus modes on your phone and computer
- Check email and messages at designated times rather than continuously
- Use separate devices or browser profiles for work and personal use

## Chapter 7: Self-Awareness: Recognizing—and Embracing—Our ADHD Differences

### The Self-Awareness Deficit in ADHD

Self-awareness — the ability to observe one's own behavior, performance, and impact on others from a somewhat objective perspective — is significantly impaired in ADHD. This is one of the most consequential executive function deficits, and one of the least discussed.

Self-awareness requires the prefrontal cortex to monitor one's own cognitive and behavioral processes — to step back from doing and observe oneself doing. When this capacity is impaired, people are less able to:

- Recognize when they are performing poorly
- Notice how their behavior is affecting others
- Identify patterns in their own conduct
- Adjust their approach when what they are doing is not working
- Accurately predict their own performance in future situations

This helps explain why people with ADHD are often surprised by feedback that seems obvious to others, why they may resist diagnoses or deny having problems that are evident to everyone around them, and why self-assessment in ADHD is systematically inflated relative to actual performance.

### Developing Self-Awareness: Six Key Skills

Research on self-awareness and ADHD has identified several specific skills that, when developed, meaningfully improve self-awareness:

**1. Self-monitoring:** The ability to check in on one's own behavior and performance during a task, not just after it. People with ADHD tend to evaluate their performance retrospectively, if at all. Learning to pause and ask "How am I doing?" during tasks — using external cues like timers — can develop this skill.

**2. Perspective-taking:** The ability to view oneself from the outside, as others might see one. This is cultivated through feedback — genuinely solicited, openly received — and through practices like journaling, therapy, and reflection.

**3. Recognizing patterns:** Seeing across multiple instances of behavior rather than treating each incident in isolation. Keeping logs, reviewing journals, and working with coaches or therapists who track patterns over time supports this.

**4. Calibration:** Adjusting self-assessments based on feedback and evidence. This requires willingness to have one's self-perception challenged and a degree of intellectual humility.

**5. Emotional labeling:** Being able to accurately identify and name one's emotional states. Many people with ADHD have significant difficulty naming what they are feeling — a characteristic sometimes described as **alexithymia**. Developing a richer emotional vocabulary supports self-awareness.

**6. Body awareness:** Attending to physical signals — tension, fatigue, restlessness, hunger — that carry information about one's internal state. People with ADHD often have reduced interoceptive awareness, contributing to poor self-regulation.

### Goal-Setting with Self-Awareness

Self-awareness is foundational to effective goal-setting. Without an accurate picture of where you currently are — your genuine strengths and limitations, your real patterns of behavior, your actual history of follow-through — goals tend to be either unrealistically ambitious or irrelevantly safe.

**ADHD-informed principles for goal-setting:**

- **Start with honest assessment.** Not what you wish were true about yourself, but what is demonstrably true. Use data: past performance, feedback from others, patterns across time.
- **Aim for growth, not perfection.** Goals that aim to eliminate ADHD symptoms entirely are bound to produce shame and abandonment. Goals that aim to improve by a meaningful increment are achievable.
- **Break goals into behaviors.** "Be more organized" is not a behavioral goal. "Create and use a dedicated inbox for mail" is. Behavioral specificity is essential for ADHD.
- **Build in review and adjustment.** ADHD makes it easy to lose track of goals entirely. Scheduled weekly reviews that explicitly assess progress and adjust plans keep goals alive.
- **Account for the time blindness factor.** ADHD brains systematically underestimate how long change takes. Build in realistic timeframes and plan for setbacks.

### Self-Esteem and ADHD

Chronic self-esteem difficulties are among the most consistent and painful psychological consequences of ADHD. They are not the result of clinical depression, though depression is a common co-occurring condition. They are the result of decades of accumulated evidence, from the perspective of the person with ADHD, that they do not measure up.

The evidence accumulates from childhood: the teacher's impatient sigh, the parent's frustrated reminder, the friends who moved on, the opportunities missed, the promises broken, the potential unrealized. By adulthood, many people with ADHD carry an internal narrative — "I am unreliable," "I am irresponsible," "I'm not as smart as people think," "I always let people down" — that feels like fact because it seems to be supported by a lifetime of experience.

**Strategies for rebuilding self-esteem:**

- **Challenge the narrative.** Work with a therapist to examine the evidence for and against the most damaging self-beliefs. Are they really supported by the evidence, or has the evidence been selectively filtered?
- **Identify genuine strengths.** People with ADHD often have significant strengths that their self-narrative discounts or ignores: creativity, humor, generosity, resilience, passion, the ability to hyperfocus on things that matter. These are real and deserve recognition.
- **Reframe the history.** Many ADHD-related failures look different when viewed through a neurological lens rather than a moral one. You were not failing because you were bad. You were failing because you had an undiagnosed, untreated neurological condition.
- **Build a success record.** Deliberately and systematically create experiences of success, however small, to begin counteracting the accumulated record of failure. Adjust goals to your actual current capacities so that success is achievable.
- **Seek out community.** Being around other adults with ADHD — in support groups, online communities, or social circles — provides perspective, reduces shame, and builds identity.

### Positive Self-Talk for ADHD Adults

Internal self-talk in adults with ADHD is often harsh, critical, and contemptuous in a way that would be recognized as abusive if directed at another person. People say things to themselves that they would never say to a friend: "You're so stupid," "You can't do anything right," "Why are you like this?"

This internal voice is not simply a personality trait. It is partly the internalized voices of others — parents, teachers, peers — whose frustration and disappointment were absorbed over years. It is also maintained by the ongoing experience of ADHD impairment, which seems to confirm the self-criticism moment by moment.

**Approaches to developing a more compassionate inner voice:**

- **Self-compassion practice** (Kristin Neff's work): Treating yourself with the same kindness and understanding you would offer a good friend in the same situation
- **CBT for self-critical thoughts:** Identifying the automatic self-critical thoughts, examining their evidence and logic, and replacing them with more balanced alternatives
- **Reparenting:** In therapy, exploring the origins of the critical voice and developing a more nurturing internal perspective
- **Affirmations:** For some people, deliberately rehearsing more accurate and compassionate self-statements helps over time. The key is that the statements must feel at least somewhat plausible, not just wishfully positive.

### Social Anxiety and ADHD

Social anxiety is significantly more common in adults with ADHD than in the general population. This makes clinical sense: a lifetime of social missteps — saying the wrong thing, missing social cues, oversharing, interrupting, forgetting names, losing the thread of conversations — creates a rich history from which to derive anxiety about social situations.

Social anxiety in ADHD is often not the primary diagnosis but a consequence of years of social difficulty. The person has learned, from experience, that social situations are likely to result in embarrassing mistakes, and they have developed anticipatory anxiety accordingly.

**The social challenge of ADHD:**

- Missing nonverbal cues that others read automatically
- Difficulty following the rhythm and timing of conversation
- Talking too much about topics of personal interest
- Oversharing personal information before appropriate trust has developed
- Interrupting others and losing one's place in group conversations
- Forgetting what others have shared, which can feel dismissive or uncaring
- One-upping others' stories — sharing a similar experience in order to connect — which neurotypical people often read as self-centered

### "Peopling Is Not Getting Easier"

*By an ADDitude contributor*

We were awkward children, and we grow into awkward adults. No wonder we have social anxiety. We are anxious about social situations because we are not good at social situations. They leave us confused and hurt. We start talking, and people talk over us. We don't know when to talk. We don't know how much to talk. It's mentally exhausting.

One of the hardest things I've learned about neurotypical social behavior: if someone tells you a story, the expected response is to validate it, not to share a related story of your own. Neurotypical people experience the related story as an attempt to hijack the conversation. For most of us with ADHD, sharing a related story is how we say "I understand you, here's how." We mean connection. They read competition.

I don't want to be neurotypical. I like myself the way I am. But in some social situations, understanding how neurotypical people expect interactions to work — and being able to adapt to that — saves a lot of exhaustion and anxiety.

### "To My Kids: A Love Letter from Your Scatterbrained, Unpredictable, Truly Lucky Mom"

*By Susie B. Cross*

I have some neurotypical mom friends, and frankly, I don't get them.

They have dedicated junk drawers filled with a couple of errant paper clips and their coupon folios; I have junk closets, stuffed with unfinished craft projects, Happy Meal toys, and detritus from the early 2000s. Those mythological moms seemingly float through life with their clothes right-side-out. Other moms with ADHD? I get them, and they get me.

We are smart, savvy, and resourceful — just out-of-sync with the neurotypical crowd. Like so many other moms with ADHD, I bump and rattle through life, and my mothering style doesn't quite qualify as mainstream, but I'm no less (or more) a parent.

My kids are routinely late for school. How am I supposed to know where my car keys are every single day?! But I also may deliver them pathologically early — because I get overly anxious about them never being on time. My boys also arrive at unanticipated times to unanticipated places.

In my house, the food groups get tossed out in favor of tepid plates of whatever sludge I can dig up. The dinner window spans from about 4:30 p.m. until 9-ish — and things get even more interesting if I zone out and forget that providing an evening meal is part of my job description. We call those "scrounge" nights.

I can't remember many things — and my boys know that. Simple verbal reminders lose effectiveness when I daydream about beach rocks or how good fabric softener smells. My boys tape permission slips to the light fixture above the kitchen table, and they always check their lunchboxes before walking out the door — to ensure I have not packed them a can of condensed soup or my wallet. I've trained them well.

After so many years of kicking myself, I am somewhat at peace with my ADHD-inspired foibles. After all, I have had this brain for 55 years, and it's not going anywhere. I know these near-men have learned a few things by having me as their mom. I pray they understand that nurturing and loving are not in the details. My executive functions may fall short, but I exceed at keeping it real, adding a little unpredictable spice to every day, and giving bear hugs. Equally as important, I hope they realize what took me so many years to grasp: every brain is different, and a scattered one is just as lovely as any other.

*Susie B. Cross is an ADDitude contributor and blogger.*
