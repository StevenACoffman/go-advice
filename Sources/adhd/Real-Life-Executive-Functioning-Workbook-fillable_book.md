# Introduction

We begin our executive functioning guidebook with a story:

Frustrated and angry, Sam yells at her teacher. “You’re just another person out to get me!” storms out of the classroom, bumping another student hard into the wall on her way out the door.  As Sam storms down the hallway, she knows her teacher is already calling Andy or Joan.

#### Executive Functioning in 'real-life'

For Sam, what started as an issue with procrastination led to an aggressive behavior likely to get her in trouble at school. Sam has some excellent skills to build on, but her procrastination gets in the way. Over time, organization, time management, and planning challenges contribute to deteriorated relationships with her family members, teachers, and peers.

It's easy to try to pinpoint Sam's troubles on a diagnosis, on her academics, or her struggling social skills.  But what if there's another explanation for Sam's procrastination?  Like so many other students with unique learning needs, Sam's underlying challenges may relate to executive functioning or how she organizes, plans, and completes goals.  The reason for Sam's struggles is not her 'bad behavior' or lack of social skills.  It's a 'real-life' problem with executive functioning.

#### Teaching Executive Functioning

If we know that boosting executive functioning skills can help reduce the barriers to success for many students, then why don't we spend time teaching these behaviors at a young age? Unfortunately, with so much emphasis on academic performance and meeting IEP goals, for many non-traditional learners, executive functioning falls through the cracks.  Most schools emphasize executive functioning as secondary skills, taught through repeated experience with academic-based projects.  Rarely do schools dedicate time to a curriculum or directly teaching executive functioning behaviors.

## Planning

Before launching into the first chapter, take a few minutes to consider how many tools you've already used today to plan your life.  Three?  Seven?  One extra ambitious reader once told us they used more than 30 different tools to help order the day, plan out tasks, and stay on track.

Our environment is filled with devices to keep us on time (watches, smartphones, calendars, etc.) and on track (folders, labels, checklists, etc.) with the demands of daily life.  Thousands of different planning and organization tools are available at our fingertips. Many of these tools we've integrated so seamlessly into our daily life we rarely even stop to consider how they keep our behaviors in check.

But none of these tools are helpful if we never learn the planning skills to use them in the first place.  Among all the different executive functioning skills to teach teens and young adults with unique learning needs, planning can be the most helpful to learn first.

As parents, we want our children to recognize and perform daily tasks efficiently without many challenges or disruptions. As teachers, we see value in how planning ties directly into problemsolving and academic achievement. However, when our children struggle with planning, we see the breakdowns, frustrations, escape, and avoidance behaviors, and in some cases, even severe problem behaviors like aggression and self-harm. Therefore, it’s essential that parents and teachers not only understand the ins and outs of planning but also understand how deficiencies in these and other executive functioning skills might be causing broader behavioral challenges for your child.

#### What Are Planning Skills?

Planning skills fall into a group of executive functioning behaviors related to a child’s ability to identify and manage future-orientated tasks. Planning behaviors involve how an individual identifies future responsibilities and events, sets goals to complete them, and analyzes the steps needed to complete the task ahead of time. Planning skills are often closely tied to the executive functioning skills of organizing and prioritizing. We use planning along with these other skills to gather information or resources and assemble it into main ideas and processes. When we use planning with organization, we have the right information and tools and know what to do with them to solve a problem.

#### Why Are Planning Skills So Important?

Once you understand the basics of planning skills, it quickly becomes apparent why parents and teachers highly value them and how they tie to the success of children, teens, and young adults. Studies show that executive functioning skills like planning and organization directly correlate to a variety of different performance indicators, including:

#### Examples of Planning Skills

Increased academic performance and thoughts about one’s own academic achievement (Bailey, Andrzejewski, Greif, Svingos & Heaton, 2018; Jacobson & Parkinson, 2015) Successful social engagement with family members and peers (Dawson & Guare, 2018) Employment and occupational success later in life (Barkley and Fischer, 2011)

#### Planning Skills and Problem Behavior

Deficits in executive functioning skills like planning can also influence problem behaviors and how others perceive a child’s problem behavior. Can you or your child identify with any of these scenarios?

Your child can complete tasks fully, but only if there’s a clear, step-by-step organization. Your child experiences meltdowns or has problem behaviors when tasks don’t go smoothly when things feel out of control, or they hit bumps in the middle of a task. You frequently find your child missing the right materials at the right time, including leaving for school or work without all the required materials, leaving behind personal belongings at friends' and family members' homes, etc.

Creating and following daily schedules creates conflicts for you and your child. Others are responsible for planning, coordinating, and following through with social events with your child’s friends and family. Your child can identify the end steps of a goal but struggles to determine the initial point to get started or intermediate steps that need to happen first.

In each of these scenarios, the problem behavior and frustration could be eliminated or substantially reduced by reducing the failures in planning behavior.

#### Teaching Planning Skills and the Planning Skills Progression

just like many other skills for unique learners, every individual will have strengths and areas of need when it comes to planning skills.  Understanding where to begin teaching planning skills is important to avoid additional frustration and problem behavior.  The planning skills of a fiveyear-old may be different from a young adult.  But if your child is missing some of the foundational planning skills at a young age, more advanced skills may not be possible to teach without backing up to the basics first.

The key is to identify component goals and treat them as a slow progression towards a wide range of planning skills.  Consider this sample progression of component goals one might teach starting with the skill of selecting a meal and moving all the way to complex meal planning for special occasions.

When you consider planning skills in a progression, it becomes more clear that a child who struggles to plan meals for an entire day lacks the foundational skills to evaluate nutrition or select new recipes. By starting with smaller, foundational goals and following a progression forward to more challenging steps, you're setting yourself and your child up for more success.

Some steps in a planning skills progression may come easier than others. That's okay.

Remember that long list you created earlier of the different tools you use for planning and organization? We use these tools to help at times when planning may be more challenging. If your child is struggling with a particular component goal in the progression, keep working at it.

### Task Analysis Generator

#### Skill Level:

#### Background:

Task analysis is the process of breaking a larger skill down into component sub-tasks and teaching each simpler step as a building block to a larger goal.  Creating a task analysis can be a helpful tool during the planning stages of a household chore or project with many different steps.  It can also be helpful to overcome struggles with task initiation since the next step or sub-task is already laid out for the learner.

#### Materials:

#### Instructions:

Download and print the Task Analysis Printable Worksheet (p.20). Write down the ending objective at the top of the task analysis.  Task Analysis works well for academic tasks, household chores, or any project to break apart into different sub-tasks.   Tasks that work well for a task analysis should have multiple steps and sub-tasks with a discrete skill (i.e., clear start and endpoint.) Break down the task into simple sub-tasks.  It can be helpful to do the task yourself and record each step as you go, or have a second person perform the task.  Observe that person complete each step of the task and write down what they do. Add visuals (optional). Some learners will benefit from visuals paired with the written instructions.  Take photos or use an app-based tool to generate a corresponding visual or symbol representing each step. Confirm that the steps are effective in reaching the end outcome.  Having a second person follow the steps in your written task analysis.  Identify any areas where they may have stumbled or needed clarification to complete a sub-task.  Revise if needed. Implement the task analysis. Place the task analysis in an easy-to-reference location. Each time the learner needs to complete the activity, practice following along with the step-by-step guide.

#### Mastery Criteria:

Generating a task analysis can take practice, even for professionals.  Start with simpler tasks (5 or fewer steps) first until you are comfortable and fluent with the procedure.  Then move on to more complex tasks with more steps.  Consider this exercise mastered when you can generate a task analysis for new projects.

### Daily Homework Planner

#### Background:

#### Skill Level:

Many students report the hardest part of homework is getting organized.  Knowing what needs to be done when, what tasks need to be completed first, and where to start can be debilitating for some of us.  Instead of stumbling to the point of frustration, start each homework session with a simple planning exercise. Review the tasks, budget time, and create priorities so there's a clear road map to help you get started.

#### Materials:

#### Instructions:

Download and print a copy of the Daily Homework Planner Worksheet.  You may also copy the template into your Sandbox notebook (see Exercise #6) before starting each homework session. Begin by making a list for each of the school subjects you may have homework for.  Being in the habit of listing out each subject, even if there are no immediate deadlines, can ensure that you don't inadvertently forget something on the list. For each subject list out the assigned readings, tasks, or projects in the "Assignments" section. Whenever possible,  list homework assignments with multiple parts with each sub-step separately. Next, make a list of the materials you'll need to complete the reading, task, or project.  Use notes like "math textbook" or "graphing paper" to help you gather materials later. Under "Time", create a time estimate for how much time it will take to complete each task on the list. Don't worry about the exact timing, but this will allow you to budget the right amount of tasks for the time available in your homework session. Under "Priorities" decide which order to complete tasks in.  Some people find it helpful to start with the shortest tasks first to build momentum.  Others find it helpful to mix up the order of easier and harder tasks. When the worksheet is completed, gather all of the materials on the materials list.  Start with the first item in the priorities list and work through all of the homework tasks in order. Check off each task as you go.

#### Expansion Ideas:

#### Mastery Criteria: Some Learners May Need Assistance to Develop a Homework Plan. Others May Find It Easier to Work Through a Plan Once It's Developed for Them. Work Towards Being Able to Create and Execute the Daily Homework Planner Without Support Each Day for One Week.

# Chapter Two: Time Management

## Time Management

Among all of the resources available to us, time is the great equalizer. We all have the same amount afforded us each day, and no amount of wealth or skill can create more time. That being said, how we manage and utilize our time can make a tremendous difference in our productivity and happiness. It’s so important to us that we spend a significant amount of time and effort in learning how to efficiently and effectively manage our time.

According to The Productivity Institute, the average American uses 13 different resources to help manage and control their time (Wetmore, 2018).  Teens and young adults with diverse learning needs also need time management skills. Practical time management skills are learned behavior, taught through a variety of different learning experiences as we grow and become more independent.

When working with children, teens, and young adults with unique learning needs, the way we teach time management may look different, but the skills are just as important to overall success.

#### What Are Time Management Skills?

Time management refers to a broad set of skills related to understanding time and how to use time effectively. According to leading executive functioning researchers, Dr. Peg Dawson and Dr. Richard Guare (2018), time management skills relate to how we effectively use our time and how that use impacts ourselves and others around us.

Children and adults with good time management skills not only understand how long tasks will take, but they’re also able to budget time effectively and complete routines with ease. Time management includes our capacity to estimate time, allocate time, and stay within time limits and deadlines.

#### Why Are Time Management Skills So Important?

Researchers have carefully examined how time management skills, along with other executive functioning skills, relate to academic, vocational, and social success. It’s also been well documented that students with diverse learning needs often struggle with time concepts and have poorly developed time management (Manganello, 1994).

Once you understand some of the benefits of time management skills, it becomes clear why parents and teachers pursue ways to teach these skills at home and in the classroom. Children with well-developed time-management skills have higher academic achievement, experience improved economic benefits of stable employment, have a higher quality of life, and improved social relationships.

#### Time Management and Problem Behavior

Not only is time management important for academic, vocational, and social independence, but also deficits in time management can connect closely to a child’s challenging behaviors.

## Task Initiation

We demand more from teens and young adults than ever before. When we demand it in an environment with more distractions than we can count, challenges with task initiation become even more apparent. In truth, all parents navigate some amount of stalling, delay tactics, and the occasional need for prompting. And although we want our kids to rush off to complete their chores, homework, and work responsibilities without reminders, we know that sometimes parenting requires those gentle reminders to help our children succeed. But raising teens and young adults that struggle with task initiation can be one of the most challenging and frustrating aspects of parenting.

For decades researchers have studied how individuals with unique learning needs show differences in executive functioning skills like task initiation (Dawson & Guare, 2018; Kenworthy, et al., 2008). These differences in the ability to get started with tasks can translate into challenges with various daily living skills and social relationships. Therefore, parents need to understand how task initiation skills relate to a child's learning and independence.

#### What Are Task Initiation Skills?

Task initiation involves a child’s ability to initiate and independently start a task. It can include various skills, including independently generating ideas, solving problems, and responding to instructions without needing others’ support. Along with time management and planning, task

#### What Happens When We Teach Task Initiation?

By addressing task initiation deficits, individuals get a two-fold benefit. First, they’re apt to encounter fewer aversives in the form of ‘nagging’ parents, redirections, and physical or verbal prompting. And because they complete tasks independently, individuals also likely encounter higher rates of positive reinforcements like praise, success with goals, and tangible reinforcers. The more quickly a child learns to complete tasks, the faster they can move on to preferred activities, ultimately reducing the time everyone spends dealing with problem behavior.

#### Identifying Task Initiation Goals

Arguably, task initiation should be one of the first executive functioning skills to focus on because it closely aligns with other skills like planning, time management, and emotional control. If you can’t independently get started with tasks, many other related executive functioning behaviors are challenging to teach.

One approach to teaching task initiation is to focus on the skill in the form of task analysis. Task analysis is the process of breaking a larger skill down into component ‘sub-tasks and teaching each simpler step as a building block to a larger goal. As the learner accomplishes each smaller milestone, they eventually become more independent at completing the final goal.

Task analysis methods are widely accepted as evidence-based practice (EBP) and used among special education, behavior analysts, and other therapy professionals. Task analyses work well for simple skills like putting on a jacket but more complex behavior goals like task initiation and completion. As an example, review the goals below related to a step-by-step teaching of task initiation. ## Organization

When it comes to learning the basics about organization skills, the numbers say it all.

According to the National Association of Productivity and Organizing Professionals (NAPO, 2020)

Americans spend more than $4.36 billion annually on home storage and organization products. On average, we lose up to nine items per day. That means we spend time looking for 198,743 items over our lifetime. 36% of young adults say they feel judged about how organized they appear. 38% feel they would advance in their career if they could become more organized.

Now consider that teens and young adults with unique learning needs often struggle with executive functioning skills like organization more than traditional learners.  The challenges quickly add up. It is why organizational skills are among the essential tools you can teach your teen or young adult today to set the stage for future success.

#### What Are Organization Skills?

Organization involves how we gather stimuli in our environment to complete tasks quickly and more effectively. It’s how we arrange our environment to provide order and structure to the items and activities around us. Organization skills are often closely tied to our ability to plan and manage our time.

We certainly may know from experiencing a messy bedroom or a backpack in disarray what disorganized looks like, but defining organization can mean different things to different individuals.

#### How We Develop Organization Skills

We begin to develop organization skills early in life and progress towards more complex organizing behaviors as our responsibilities increase.  Depending on the skills and strengths of your individual learner, teaching organization skills might involve teaching skills like:

Sorting and categorizing – identifying, describing, and grouping objects by size, shape, pattern, function, and form. Putting things in designated places – when finished with materials, placing items back where they belong. Gather materials to complete a task - learning where items belong, finding what's needed for a project, and creating a plan to succeed. Use reminders and organization systems – learning to use a planner, visual schedule, digital and paper reminders, checklists, and other tools. How to reorganize if needed – recognizing when our environment is disorganized, when we need to pause for a reset, and how to get back on track with better organization.

#### Why Are Organization Skills So Important?

Without a basic level of organization, it wouldn’t take long for the world as we know it to deteriorate. We rely on an organized way of life in our schools, our homes, and our workplaces. Research shows that individuals who have good organization skills and implement them effectively have better outcomes across many different well-being areas. When things are disorganized, we report higher rates of distraction and stress (NAPO, 2020). Students who lack organization skills have more academic challenges, experience lower grades, and negative interactions with teachers (Anderson, et al., 2008).

#### Examples of Organization Skills

Orders items or pictures into sequential order. Tells an organized story with a beginning, middle, and end. Returns items back to where they belong with a visual cue or drawing. Cleans up at the end of an activity or the end of the day. Maintains toys, personal items, and workspace in a neat and orderly manner. Arrives at school or work with the required materials. Keeps homework and essential documents in good condition. Develops an outline or list to organize tasks and topics systematically.

For many teens and young adults with unique learning needs, organizational skills must be taught explicitly through targeted interventions. Yet even modest improvements in organizational skills can decrease symptoms of inattention and increase academic performance (Bikic, et al., 2017).  Learners with developmental learning differences get faster at basic tasks and have fewer errors when using systems to stay organized (Dormany, et al., 2009).

#### Organization Skills and Problem Behavior

If we know that organizational skills show many benefits to our teens and young adults, what happens when those skills are underdeveloped or lagging?

Do you find your child’s belongings disordered and then have to ‘fight’ to get them to clean up? Does your child use problem behavior to get help searching for assignments, work materials, or belongings? Does your child look for ways to get out of organizing, cleaning, or planning tasks? Do they sometimes go to extremes like meltdowns or aggression because they can’t find what they need?

While organization means different things for different individuals, keeping belongings and tasks tidy can ultimately reduce problem behaviors. Organizational skills result in less frustration and a reduced need to use problem behaviors to recruit adult help or escape a disordered task. Organizational skills create a sense of control, which can frequently be a positive reinforcer for teens and young adults with unique learning needs. Tasks finish more quickly and efficiently, leading to more time to engage in preferred activities.

#### The Difference Between Neat and Messy

When we get started working on organization skills, one of the first things we ask and evaluate is if the child knows the difference between a clean, organized space and a messy one. It sounds simple, but some individuals with unique learning needs might not evaluate their the environment in the same way you do.

Start by pulling up some images online and ask your child to identify spaces that look clean and organized. Show some photos of unusually messy rooms, some cluttered but organized, and some images of areas that might need some tidying up. Then move to spaces in your household–you might even need to make some messes of your own!) Can your child identify and discriminate the differences? If not, it’s not likely that you’ll be able to agree on what it means to be organized until you do. Keep working to establish useful definitions of what it means to be organized that both you and your learner can relate to.

# Chapter Five: Problem Solving

EXERCISES TO BUILD PROBLEM-SOLVING SKILLS

## Problem Solving

When parents reach out to us about working with a child, invariably, we end up asking about problem-solving skills. Questions like:

Does your child identify problems? When you see a problem in the making, can your child see it too? If your child comes to you with a problem, how much effort does it take them to generate a reasonable solution? Can your child evaluate the pros and cons of different strategies to solve a problem? When your child eventually finds a solution to a problem, can they see implement it and stay the course to see the problem through?

For many of the parents and teachers we work with, it becomes clear by asking these questions that the struggles and challenging behaviors they encounter with their child aren't related to "undisciplined" or "lazy" kids.  They're not related to bad parenting either.  Instead, we find many challenges our unique learners face relate to poorly developed problem-solving skills.

#### What Are Problem Solving Skills?

Problem-solving involves our capacity to identify and describe a problem and generate solutions to fix it. According to Newell & Simon (1972), “a person is confronted with a problem when he wants something and does not know immediately what series of actions he can perform to get it” (p.72).  We engage in problem-solving behaviors when we evaluate possible steps forward and then take action to obtain the outcome we’re looking for. Problem-solving involves many other executive functioning behaviors, including attentional control, planning, and task initiation. We need to pay attention to our

1o4

environment to notice a problem, outline different strategies, and then attempt one of those solutions. Depending on the problem, we might also need to use time management, emotional control, or organization skills. Over time, if we can observe our own behavior and understanding of the environment through working memory and self-monitoring behaviors, it can also influence our problem-solving skills.

#### Developing Problem Solving Skills

Problem-solving skills develop early through play behaviors as infants and toddlers. At these early stages of development, much of a child’s play consists of cause and effect play or ‘figuring out how things work.’ As children move into early learning years, problem-solving includes learning decision making and turn-taking. In addition, children learn to brainstorm solutions to simple problems and notice problems when others point them out.

#### Examples of Problem Solving Skills

Complete puzzles and games to accomplish a goal. Use language and body movements to achieve an outcome. Identify and define a problem, including where the problem originated and why. Break apart a problem into smaller parts. Identify problems in different social contexts, including work, school, home, and with friends. Sort out conflicts and what to do in complicated social situations. Seek guidance from others about what to do in problem situations. Persist in developing new strategies when previous attempts fail.

As children reach adolescence, they begin to independently identify problems in many settings, including home, school, work, and time with friends. They sort out conflicts and decide what steps to take but may seek adult feedback and support to evaluate the potential advantages and disadvantages of solutions. As teens and young adults, developing good problem-solving skills involves generating unique solutions to complex problems and persisting through multiple solutions until a problem resolves.

#### Problem Solving and Problem Behavior

Not all teens and young adults develop adequate problem-solving behaviors like those listed here. Learners with a history of behavioral and learning challenges may not always use good problem-solving skills to manage stressful situations (Kern, et al., 2016). We all know students who use challenging behaviors like talking back, arguing, property destruction, and aggression when presented with difficult tasks. We also know students who shut down, check out, or struggle to follow directions when they encounter new or unknown situations. Without a stepby-step model for problem-solving, including how to identify a problem and choose a replacement behavior to solve it, many of our children and students use challenging behaviors instead. Over time, learners labeled as ‘defiant,’ ‘lazy,’ or ‘helpless’ may actually just need better problem-solving tools.

#### The Ideal Problem Solving Method

The IDEAL in the IDEAL Problem-Solving Method stands for: Identify the problem. Define and describe the outcome or goal if the problem is solved. Explore possible strategies and solutions. Anticipate the outcomes and then act. Look at the outcomes and learn from them.

#### I - Identify the Problem

There’s no real way to create a solution to a problem unless you first know the scope of the problem. We have to start somewhere. Begin by identifying the issue in your own words. Then, outline the facts and the unknowns.

Examples of identifying problems:

“I have a math quiz next week and don’t know how to do the problems.” “I can’t access my distance learning course website.”

"The trash needs to be taken out, and I can't find any trash bags."

#### D - Define and Describe an Outcome

The second step in the IDEAL problem-solving process is to define an outcome or goal for what will happen when the problem is solved. Multiple people can agree that a problem exists but have very different ideas on goals or outcomes. By deciding on an outlined objective first, it can speed up the process of identifying solutions.

Defining outcomes and goals may be a difficult step for some diverse learners. The results don't need to be complicated--after all, you don't yet know which action you'll take--but the description should be clear for everyone involved.

Examples of defining outcomes:

“I want to do well on my math quiz.” “I get access to the course website.” “The trash gets taken out before the trash pickup day tomorrow.”

#### E - Explore Possible Strategies

Once you have an outcome, it's time to brainstorm solutions. All possible strategies to solve the problem should be on the table during this stage, even outlandish ones. At this stage, it can be helpful to make lists, use sticky notes or voice memos to record any ideas. Many of the exercises in The Real-Life Executive Functioning Workbook were designed to assist in developing solutions during the exploration stage, especially for individuals who might struggle with creative idea generation. If needed, consult with a trusted friend, family member, or someone familiar with the problem who might also offer solutions to the problem.

Examples of possible strategies to solve a problem:

“To prep for the quiz, I could review the textbook, ask for math help from a friend, look up the problems online, or email my teacher.” "To gain access to the course website, I could email my teacher for the course access, ask for help from a classmate, or try to reset my password." “Trash needs to go out so I could use something else for a trash bag, place an online order for bags, take the trash out without a bag, ask a neighbor for a bag, or go shopping after work today for trash bags.”

#### A - Anticipate Outcomes and Act

Once we generate a list of strategies, the next step in the IDEAL problem-solving model recommends that you review the potential steps and decide which one is the best option to use first. Learning to evaluate the pros and cons of action steps can take practice. Ask questions like, "What might happen if I take this step?" or "Does that step make me feel good about moving forward or uncertain?"

After evaluating the outcomes, the next step is to take action. Using some of what we know about task initiation (see Chapter 3), move forward even if you may not know the full result of taking action. Embody the idea that it's better to be doing something than nothing. Even if you may need to change course or something unexpected happens, it's better to take action.

#### L - Look and Learn

The final step in the IDEAL problem-solving model is to look at the results and learn from our attempts to solve a problem. We can sometimes forget this critical step, especially when working with individuals with unique learning needs. However, it's equally important to stop and reflect when problem-solving goes well ("Hey, I did a good job back there) and to acknowledge when things did not go well. If things didn't go as planned, learning from that experience can make problem-solving more efficient and effective in the future.

Ask questions like "How did that go?" and "What went well? What could I do differently next time?"

Examples of Look and Learn statements:

“I didn’t learn the problems from looking at the textbook, but it did help to call a friend. So I’ll start there next time.” “When I didn’t have access to the course website, resetting my password worked. I'll remember that the next time I can't get into a website.” “I ran out of trash bags because I forgot to put them on the shopping list. So I’ll buy an extra box of trash bags to have them on hand, so I don’t run out next time.”

Then, use the SMART Goal Planning Exercise (p. 1 ) to begin identifying and planning how you might accomplish problem-solving-related goals.

## Flexibility

Much of this workbook was developed and written during the 2020 COVID-19 global pandemic. If anything that lives in a pandemic showed us, it was how important it (and how difficult it can be) to adapt to unexpected change.  As every aspect of life turned upside down for parents and teachers, it quickly became apparent that some things were easy to pivot, and others were tough to change.  In times of great uncertainty and change, our skills to demonstrate flexibility made the biggest difference in how we coped and adapted to new ways of working, learning, and parenting.

#### What Is Flexibility?

While there’s no set definition of flexibility in executive functioning, we generally consider flexibility to be the behavior of switching between tasks and demands in response to changes in the environment.  In essence, it’s our ability to change our behavior to different contexts or stimuli in the environment. Researchers might use other terms to study and describe flexibility, including cognitive flexibility, shift, task switching, and mental flexibility. All of these terms relate to our ability to adapt quickly and respond to change.

Individuals who struggle with flexibility may have difficulty discriminating differences in the environment or shifting focus between different tasks or relevant information. When we’re inflexible, we may get ‘stuck’ or keep trying the same response over and over even though it’s not working.

#### Developing Flexibility

We begin to learn flexibility and develop flexible thinking early in childhood.  Toddlers and young children complete simple puzzles, games, and play that require shifting between behaviors.  As young children learn to shift from activity to activity, they manage transitions and unexpected changes without upsetting behaviors.  Then, as adolescents grow and their social networks continue to expand, they encounter more and more situations where problems and

unpredictable events occur.  In these situations, adults are close by to support and provide scaffolding as young teens adjust dynamically.

As our children grow from teenagers into young adults, we expect them to now manage unpredictable routines and meet the changing demands of school, work, and family.  We also expect teens and young adults to begin to take difficult situations in stride.  Young adults may need additional support at occasional points, but with well-developed executive functioning skills, they’re able to pivot and recover quickly when the unexpected happens.

## Working Memory

Recall from our introduction the story of Sam and her academic struggles.

“She continually asks me to repeat the instructions.” “I swear, she doesn’t remember what I tell her three minutes later.” “She gets easily frustrated and then uses problem behavior when she forgets instructions.” “Sam expects that I’m always around to help in the classroom if she can’t remember what to do.” When Sam's parents and teachers sought help for her executive functioning challenges, one of the items that came up across the board was difficulties remembering information, following instructions, and frustration when Sam couldn't get the right answer.   Given Sam's challenges with remembering and recalling information, her parents began to think she might have challenges with working memory.

#### The Basics of Working Memory

Just like a computer, our brains have a limited capacity to store and retain information.  Working memory describes our ability to remember information and recall it later when we need it later.

Working memory is commonly associated with academics like reading and math.  In part because both of those skills require us to recall information like letter sounds, sight words, and simple math facts like

addition and subtraction.  Working memory can also involve our ability to follow multiple-step instructions since we need to remember all parts of the task while completing the steps in order.

#### Working Memory and Other Executive Functioning Skills

Not only is working memory closely associated with academic success, but it's also connected to other executive functioning skills.  While we may think of it as short term recall of information, working memory also requires us to apply it to the right situations when needed. So it’s not just information retention; it's about the application as well. That’s why boosting working memory can impact other executive functioning skills as well. Studies show:

Good working memory helps us with concept formation or connecting existing ideas with new information (Cowan, 2014) Improvements in working memory can promote increases in attentional control. For example, we know that individuals who test well in working memory can more easily stay on task. Working memory can promote better impulse control and flexibility. When we have the right information at hand, we’re less likely to make knee-jerk responses.  We’re also more likely to demonstrate flexible thinking, shifting our attention and applying different rules in different settings (Center on the Developing Child at Harvard University, 2011)

#### Examples of Working Memory Tasks

Recalling numbers, letters, and words, beginning with two digits and increasing to longer strings. Delivering a message to another person (e.g., "Go tell your brother it's time for dinner.") Completing simple errands (e.g., "Go to the refrigerator and get the milk.") Playing simple memory and matching games. Completing math facts and sight word worksheets. Completing two, three, and four-step tasks without needing additional instructions. Gathering information from one source and applying it to another setting. Keeping track of personal belongings, remembering and recalling where things are placed around the home and classroom. Using visual schedules, written reminders, and picture instructions to help recall information and complete tasks. Attempting to problem-solve first if they cannot recall the instruction before asking for help. Demonstrating self-control of problem behaviors if they require more help to remember an instruction.

#### Nature Or Nurture: Can You Boost Working Memory?

Like so many other psychology and child development areas, there's a debate among researchers about nature vs. nurture and working memory. Some believe we are born with a fixed working memory capacity. We inherit working memory through 'nature' or our genetics. Others believe that working memory can be 'nurtured' or increased through training and practice.

In part, this debate stems from the problems inherent in studying working memory. Individuals improve when given tools to boost working memory-related tasks like math, reading, and recalling factual information in both laboratory and applied settings. Some researchers question whether improvements we see are simply 'practice effects' or enhancements because of repeatedly doing the same tasks. Other researchers describe these improvements as an actual expansion in working memory.

But for all practical purposes, what’s important is that there's an improvement. So even if we can't yet settle the nature vs. nurture debate, we can still help teens boost these skills.

#### Strategy Training and Core Training

There are two main ways to boost working memory skills: strategy training and core training. Both have shown promise in clinical and applied settings at helping learners improve how they remember information and use it in new environments.

Core training often includes a broad approach, with many different tasks and stimuli included in the training program. Practicing a diversity of skills at a high frequency potentially increases the likelihood of working memory gains.

#### Other Strategies for Working Memory

In addition to strategy and core training, there are other options for students with unique learning needs.

1.Break it down. Individuals with working memory challenges struggle with retaining complex instructions or lots of information at once. If you know your learner has difficulty managing lots of information at once, adjust your expectations and break steps down. Provide instructions step-by-step, or have your learner complete smaller components of a task and recruit feedback more frequently. Breaking tasks down may seem like taking a step backward, but ultimately, if you give your teen or young adult too much information and the process breaks down, you'll have to provide help anyway. Sometimes, less is more.

2.Reduce distractions. Some working memory training exercises focus solely on helping individuals tune out irrelevant information and distractions. If you know your learner is heading into a complex task that demands full attention, an effective strategy to support success is to reduce as many distractions as possible. Turn off devices and screens. Play white noise or background ambient sounds to drown out conversations. By giving your teen or young adult fewer things to tune out, you increase the probability they'll stay focused on the task at hand.

3.Offer breaks. Just as we look to break down tasks into simpler components to promote success, we can also help learners that struggle with working memory with more frequent breaks. Getting up to stretch, get a drink of water, or use the restroom can make a big difference for some learners who have difficulty with an intense focus on complex tasks. For some learners who have good working memory skills but struggle with the motivation to apply them in academic or vocational settings, more frequent breaks can improve performance on tedious or lesspreferred tasks.

## Emotional Control

Let's think back again to our introduction story of Sam and her outburst at school.

Frustrated and angry, Sam yells at her teacher. “You’re just another person out to get me!” storms out of the classroom, bumping another student hard into the wall on her way out the door. As Sam storms down the hallway, she knows her teacher is already calling the principal…

The example above showcases a breakdown in emotional control. Sam’s response to her teacher’s feedback and not getting to relax at the end of class happens in thousands of homes and classrooms every day. As students like Sam grow into adulthood, the negative consequences for challenges in emotional control only increase.

As a parent, educator, or coach, what we know about emotional control can be a game-changer in promoting success and achievement in students like Sam.

#### What Is Emotional Control?

Sometimes known as emotional regulation, emotional control describes how we manage and respond to emotional experiences in the environment. Experiencing emotions can include sensations in the body and the thoughts that go along with them.  We know that teens and young adults experience both positive emotions

(excitement, joy, happiness, pride, etc.) and negative emotions (disappointment, sadness, guilt, frustration, overwhelm, etc.) It's what we do about those emotional states that become important. Learning to respond to positive and negative emotions is the essence of emotional control.

#### Why Emotional Control Is Important

Research supports that emotional control is among the most critical skills necessary for children and teens to grow into healthy and independent adults. According to the University of

#### Examples of Emotional Control

When teens and young adults fail to demonstrate good emotional control and regulation, it can also cause negative impacts, including:

Labeling basic emotions in self and in others. Using conversations or statements to express emotions and actions. Learning to control upset behavior, tantrums, and meltdowns. Describing the sensations of emotions in the body. Labeling advanced and subtle emotions in self and others including empathy, suffering, affection, disappointment, etc. Using perspective-taking to see another’s point of view. Talking or writing about past events and reflecting on how to manage emotions in the future. Talking to a trusted friend about challenges and concerns. Noticing and asking when it’s necessary to take a break.

Avoiding work or school activities. Excessive social media use. Procrastinating on other responsibilities. Physical or verbal aggression. Self-injury or self-harm. Abusing food, alcohol, or other substances.

#### Emotional Control and Problem Behavior

While the behaviors above might happen after a stressful event, individuals with unique learning needs may also have heightened responses at the moment. Ask yourself if any of these problem behaviors related to emotional control describe your child:

Does your child have frequent outbursts? Get in trouble at school for blurting, hyperactivity, or outof-seat behavior when excited? Over-reactions or violent/aggressive behavior when upset? Does your child struggle to bounce back after a setback? Engage in high rates of negative thoughts?

All of these may contribute to a need for increased development of emotional control. By improving emotional control skills, your child can begin to use strategies other than problem behavior to respond to emotions. While in some ways, it is developmentally appropriate for children and teens to rely on adult support for emotional control. However, developing greater emotional control allows children to recognize emotions as physical sensations in the body, rather than being controlled by them or act upon them.

#### Emotional Control and Executive Functioning Skills

Improving your teen’s emotional control may ultimately mean targeting other executive functioning skills. In our introduction, for learners like Sam, emotional outbursts and problem behaviors may be related to skill deficits in planning and organization. If we address the root issues related to these other skill areas, problem behaviors decrease.

Teaching skills in time management, organization, and task initiation may be more difficult for other learners without addressing emotional control first. Learners who don't have strong skills to respond to frustration and failure may need to work on those strategies first. For these learners, focus first on keeping emotions in check and ultimately learning other executive functioning skills becomes more manageable.

Other considerations when teaching emotional control:

Emotional triggers frequently happen for children, teens & young adults. 21st-century kids have busier schedules than ever, crammed with school, work, hobbies, friends, and family. It also means their schedules are also packed full of opportunities where frustration, disappointment, and challenges emotions occur. Relationships also become more complex, and the expectations for social behaviors increase in our adolescent years. Being intentional about practicing emotional regulation skills can be an essential part of helping reduce the stress and manage the emotional triggers that often happen for our learners.

Emotional control won’t just ‘happen.’ It comes from learned experience and skillbuilding. While many parents and teachers take a "hope for the best" strategy for emotional control, research shows it's better to be intentional about helping your teen build these skills. Especially for teens with unique learning needs, who may or may not need customized interventions to learn new skills, assuming that emotional control will just ‘happen over time’ puts your teen at risk for falling behind and experiencing greater stress.

We learn about emotional regulation by watching others, talking about emotions, and getting feedback on our behavior. It takes many more learning opportunities for some teens to learn emotional control than others.

When having conversations with your teen about emotional regulation, it can be helpful to talk about real-life situations and triggers you also experience. It's easy for us to keep emotions to ourselves and not necessarily allow our teens to see the hundreds of emotional regulation responses we make throughout the day.

For example, when someone cuts you off in the parking lot for the best parking space, label the emotion and the emotional response. "Look at that! How frustrating! That wasn't very nice of that driver. But it's no big deal; I'll find another spot." Show your teen that they aren’t the only one that has difficult emotions. Narrate your emotional thoughts and how you’re handling them appropriately.

Pushing another student might mean a trip to the principal's office as a teen. Pushing another adult in the grocery store could mean criminal charges as an adult. Refusing to complete a task at home might mean losing access to screen time. Refusing to complete a task as an adult could mean getting fired from a job. Learning to manage emotions, avoid conflict, and avoid impulsive behavior while the consequences are less severe is critical for preventing more severe consequences later.

## Impulse Control

We know from research and our own personal experiences that the human brain can be easily susceptible to impulsive behavior.  Acting on impulse, sometimes making split-second decisions, comes from our evolutionary instincts to survive.  Take too long to act in the ancient world, and you might be another creature's lunch!

Unfortunately, even though our technology-driven world has rapidly evolved, our impulsive tendencies have not.  In the 21st century, we are subject to emotional responses,  decisionmaking challenges, and acting without thinking.  As parents, teachers, and coaches, we need to know how to support our learners by combating this disconnect between our instincts to act on impulse and demonstrating appropriate self-control.

#### What Is Impulse Control?

Impulse control—sometimes known as self-control or our inhibition—is an executive functioning skill that allows us to engage independently in society. Impulse control helps us “think before acting” and to prioritize long-term rewards over short-term gain. When we demonstrate appropriate impulse control behaviors, we resist or avoid doing things that may be harmful to ourselves and others.

Like many other executive functioning skills in this workbook, we are born with a genetic makeup that supports self-control, but we gain impulse control through environmental learning.  Impulse control develops in our toddler years as we learn primary safety responses like avoiding a hot stove or running into the street.

We expect toddlers to need constant monitoring for impulsive behaviors but begin to fade our support as we grow more independent in elementary and middle school. By pre-teen and teenage years, we continue some risky behaviors but often follow the safety rules and strive for social norms. By adulthood, we can manage impulsive behavior across all settings, avoiding most instances of rushing, reckless, or harmful behaviors.

#### Impulse Control & Problem Behavior

What happens if your child doesn't continue to develop good impulse control behaviors? Selfcontrol and impulse behavior might not be the cause of problem behavior itself. But, it certainly contributes to how quickly your teen or young adult learner might default back to poor habits and behaviors. Children who struggle with impulse control might demonstrate problem behaviors like:

Interrupting in conversations, talking over others, or refusing to allow others to share opinions. Difficulty sharing preferred items. Refusals, whining, or tantrums when asked to stop or transition away from something enjoyable. Arguments about setting healthy limits on food, spending money, and screen time. Engaging in dangerous or unhealthy behaviors when peers are around. Giving up easily or refusing to persist with challenging tasks.

#### Potential Goals

Understands and articulates the consequences of self-controlled vs. impulsive behavior. Understands and demonstrates selfcontrolled behavior vs. impulsive behavior in controlled situations or role-plays. Demonstrates control of personal space and voice volume when around others. Maintains healthy limits on food, shopping, and screen time. Controls instances of risky behavior when alone or with others. Avoids ‘self-sabotage’ or choices that will reduce the probability of success at home, work, or school.

#### Impulse Control Disorders

Before exploring strategies to improve impulse control, it's important to pause and recognize that the impulse control behaviors above relate to under-developed executive functioning skills.  They are different behaviors than those associated with impulse control disorders. Impulse control disorders (pyromania, kleptomania, intermittent explosive disorder, etc.) are serious medical conditions that are not likely to benefit from the strategies included in this text. Consult a medical professional if you feel your child’s impulsive behavior puts themselves or others at risk of harm.

#### Impulse Control and Executive Functioning

impulse control is closely tied to how we learn to function independently. Some teens and young adults who struggle with impulse control may also be struggling with other executive functioning skills. Evaluating planning and problem-solving skills along with flexibility and emotional control can be important when we’re also trying to work on impulsivity.

There is also a well-known body of research connecting improvements in impulse control with consistent sleep and maintaining a healthy diet and exercise. Supporting your learner through the exercises in this chapter may also include working to reducing stress levels, promoting a regular, consistent sleep schedule, and engaging in physical activity as a family. Even tiny changes in habits in these areas can make a meaningful difference in improving self-control.

## Attentional Control

As of this writing, if you enter the search words "How to Focus Better" into Google, you'll encounter about 6.2 billion results. In a world with so many distractions, we scramble to sustain our attention on what matters the most. Studies show we face distractions once every 7-8 minutes throughout the day, equating to about 60 distractions in an eight-hour work or school day. These distractions can cause frustration, time management issues, and challenges in obtaining goals.

If adults struggle with this skill, imagine how difficult it can be for learners with unique needs.  Thankfully, there are some simple and easy ways you can start building attentional control in your child or student's regular daily routine. Building focus and concentration are all about identifying what works for a particular learning style, effectively practicing the skills, and celebrating successes as they come.

#### What Is Attentional Control?

Attentional control involves our ability to focus on certain stimuli in the environment while not focusing or "tuning out" other things. There are two distinct behaviors involved in attentional control: paying attention to a stimulus in the environment and ignoring or non-attending to others.  Attentional control works closely with other executive functioning skills, including working memory (using what we know to make decisions), emotional control (regulating our behavior), and cognitive flexibility (shifting focus between different stimuli in the environment).

#### Measuring Attentional Control

Instead, we often measure attentional control by directly observing a secondary, alternative behavior. These bi-products of attention are behaviors we assume would be difficult to complete without demonstrating attentional control. Often these direct observations occur through a comprehension or skills check.

Some ways you might directly observe your learner’s behavior include:

Observational learning - Can your learner watch others' behavior and perform a similar behavior immediately after? Can your learner watch others' mistakes and avoid similar mistakes in the future? Task completion - Can your learner focus long enough to complete an activity (worksheets, puzzles, games, etc.) with multiple steps without stopping? Comprehension - Can your learner accurately answer questions about something observed, read, or spoken about?

Social interactions - Can your learner stay on topic and answer questions during conversations? Does your learner get off-topic or distracted by details in social interactions? Problem-solving - Can your learner solve simple problems, including examining details in the environment and generating a solution to move forward? Task initiation - Can your learner focus on the right tasks at the right time, knowing when and where to get started?  Can your learner continue working on the next task as soon as one is completed without getting off-track?

#### Attentional Control

Learners with attentional control deficits may engage in problem behaviors to escape tasks that take too long. For example, they may act out, tantrum, or talk back if asked to return to activities once they've been distracted. In addition, frequent reminders and redirections from adults can deteriorate social relationships with parents and teachers.  Fellow students and peers may also spend less time with easily distracted learners, taking the entire classroom off task.

By targeting and improving attentional control, learners can complete tasks faster and more efficiently. Faster completion allows learners to return to preferred activities faster.  Staying focused also means fewer errors and mistakes in their work.  All of which means less time you'll need to spend reminding or reprimanding.

#### Strategies to Build Attentional Control in Teens and Young Adults

Thankfully, working on attentional control doesn't have to be time-intensive or require lots of advanced training.  Oftentimes, some simple changes to your learner's environment can make a big difference in improving focus and concentration.  Along with the exercises in this workbook, building these supports into daily routines, parents and teachers can help ease some of the stress, frustration, and problem behavior related to attentional control.

Take frequent breaks. It may seem counterintuitive that taking a break from intense concentration can increase our ability to pay attention. However, for diverse learners who may have difficulty focusing for long periods, it can be helpful to build in more frequent breaks. Breaks should be short--no more than 3-5 minutes--but long enough to give attentional control a rest. Plan physical activity during breaks. Along with taking frequent breaks, it's helpful if those 'brain breaks' include some form of physical activity. For example, we tend to shift from concentrating on a task, to focus on our smartphone news feed or emails. Yet, these tasks also require focus and concentration, so it's not restful for your learner's brain. Instead, encourage your learner to get up and moving around during breaks. Research supports that moderate to intense physical activity can increase our concentration and focus. In addition, even brief periods of physical activity can be beneficial for diverse learners. Test out times for peak attention. Does your learner focus better right away in the morning?  Does your student need to start with some easy tasks and build momentum for longer sustained attention? Is it more challenging for your child to focus when tired, hungry, or after a long day of school/work? For learners who are likely to need long-term supports to sustain attention, it's critical to examine and understand the times when attentional skills are at their peak. Choose these times for the most challenging tasks and save things requiring less concentration when focusing wains.

4. Practice focus in a designated location. Sometimes we can train our brains into focusing better just by practicing attention in the same location. Have your learner use a designated place for tasks that require sustained attention (e.g., quiet reading in the "reading corner," homework station, or workbench). Not only can you reduce the number of distractions in that particular environment, but your learner's brain will identify this location as an environment for focused attention over time.

5. Use visual timers. Sometimes helping a learner best focus in their time is just about assisting them in understanding time in general. Some evidence supports that learners who struggle with focused attention also struggle with understanding time and time management concepts. Try using a visual timer for a learner, including a countdown or time remaining marker. Learners can better conceptualize time passing by using a visual timer that indicates the total time needed to complete a task and how much time is remaining.

#### What Is Self-monitoring?

The executive functioning skill of self-monitoring reflects how we understand our behaviors and how we adjust to make changes for the future (American Psychological Association, 2020). Self-monitoring can include work-checking behaviors (reviewing mistakes, fixing errors, etc.) and social behaviors (responding to social norms, situational awareness, apologizing).

Self-monitoring can also refer to a behavior intervention technique used with diverse learners to help them record and monitor their behavior to change some aspects of responding. The executive functioning exercises in this book primarily focus on this second aspect of self-monitoring. However, implementing these techniques can improve all aspects of self-monitoring and other executive functioning skills.

#### Quantitative Vs. Qualitative Selfmonitoring

Self-monitoring involves not only observing our behaviors but also how they impact others around us. There are two primary ways we engage in selfmonitoring, depending on the type of observations we make:

Qualitative Monitoring – observing the characteristics of our environment and behavior. Attending to how things make us feel (“I get angry when…”), sensations in the body (“My neck

gets sore when I…”), or how our behavior changes the environment (“When I do this, my friends laugh at me.”). Monitoring how our behavior matches with social norms and standards.

Quantitative Monitoring – In contrast, quantitative monitoring involves direct observation of instances of behavior or permanent products of our behavior. This might include frequency (“I did twelve math problems.”), duration (“I did homework for thirty-three minutes.”) and intensity or difficulty of a behavior (“I did the extra-hard math problems.”)

#### Benefits of Learning Self-monitoring

One reason parents and teachers who work with learners with unique needs value self-monitoring is the extensive research supporting its effectiveness (The Iris Center, 2008). Individuals who engage in high rates of self-monitoring demonstrate:

Parents and teachers are not the only ones who see benefits from learning self-monitoring behaviors.  Teens and young adults with unique learning needs also report positive outcomes and benefits after being empowered to monitor their own behavior.  Some of these additional benefits include:

Provides immediate feedback – Students using self-monitoring strategies do not need an adult to intervene once the techniques are taught. Likewise, it's not necessary to have a second person involved to indicate to the student that they are on the right track. Instead, by giving them self-monitoring tools, they provide themselves with immediate feedback, often making changes faster than a teacher or parent can. Engages students in their learning and personal development – Ever feel like you’re working harder than your learner to keep things moving forward? Selfmonitoring puts the responsibility back on the learner to achieve and maintain success. As a result, successful programs show high engagement rates and motivation because the learner is invested in their own success. Improves communication – With self-monitoring interventions, your student observes their behavior and often creates a visual product (graphs, data collection forms, app reports) that you and your learner can examine together. The data objectively guides communication rather than pointing fingers or telling a student what they did or didn't do correctly. Focuses on individual performance – Finally, there's no need to compare your student to others in self-monitoring. Instead, they're simply looking at their performance and how it improves over time. This can be especially helpful for students who struggle with how their unique learning needs might differ from classmates and peers.

#### How We Develop Self-monitoring

We begin to develop self-monitoring at a young age through imitation. By identifying how our

behavior is the same or different from others, and then adapting it to match, we learn how to self-monitor. We use self-monitoring in activities like journaling, checklists, and reflection by middle school and high school. Into adulthood, we can check our work for mistakes, monitor our performance, and work harder in the future to achieve bigger goals.

While we’ve written elsewhere in the workbook, it’s worth reiterating. Evidence supports that all individuals can make progress towards achieving executive functioning goals. Some individuals may need additional strategies and support to sustain success, especially when it comes to self-monitoring. Even if your learner may need long-term plans and supports to succeed, there's strong evidence to keep working towards achieving self-monitoring goals.

## Exercise #80

#### Skill Areas:

### Bounce Back

#### Skill Level:

#### Background:

#### Materials:

Part of self-monitoring is learning to bounce back or move forward after a setback.  If your learner struggles with making mistakes giving up easily, or needs support moving forward after a roadblock, use this active learning exercise to generate solutions to help your bounce back.

#### Instructions:

Gather your materials and identify 10-15 minutes in a quiet, distraction-free space to discuss with your learner. Identify a recent time when your learner had a setback or when your learner struggled to bounce back.  Spend a few minutes discussing what happened and what was difficult about it. In the next 3-5 minutes, brainstorm strategies that your learner may have found helpful in recovering or moving forward from that situation. Then, discuss some of the advantages and disadvantages of each strategy. Select the favorite strategies from the list, and have your learner transfer them to the surface of a small rubber basketball using a permanent marker. The next time your learner struggles to move on, encourage them to "shoot hoops" to solve the problem or bounce back.  Each time the basketball comes back to them, review the strategy written on the ball and how it might help them overcome their current challenge.

#### Expansion Ideas:

#### Mastery Criteria:

If your learner benefits from a physical learning activity like the  Bounce Back exercise, consider creating a basketball with other social skills, emotional control skills, or problem-solving skills.  When your learner needs to brainstorm what to do next, encourage them to "shoot hoops" to review the strategies on their basketballs.

Mastery criteria for this exercise include creating the basketball of strategies and implementing those strategies in difficult situations. Then, review and provide support until your learner can use the ball to brainstorm and select a solution to 3 new problems they encounter.

## Exercise #81

#### Skill Areas:

### End of the Month Review

#### Skill Level:

#### Background:

#### Materials:

Even though we emphasize proactive planning in executive functioning, it's also important for individuals learning to use executive functioning strategies to reflect backward on how planning and organization went.  Use this End of Month Review exercise to reflect on the previous month as a strategy for planning.

End of Month Review worksheet (p. 251) Previous month's calendar, behavior logs, and checklists.

#### Instructions:

Download and print the End of Month Review Worksheet (p. 251). Identify a quiet, distraction-free environment for 10-15 minutes on the worksheet to work with your learner. Begin by identifying the biggest events, activities, and projects from the previous month. Then, ask your learner to reflect on each of these tasks and evaluate how successful the outcomes were. Also, identify what your learner liked best about the previous month. Use this as a way to evaluate motivation and build more positive events into the upcoming month. Once the primary events and activities are reviewed, identify with your learner what tools were used to help them succeed with activities, along with roadblocks that hindered progress. Finally, after reflecting on the previous month, begin using the information gathered to plan for the next month.  Identify what activities your learner has to look forward to and the required tools necessary to succeed.

#### Mastery Criteria:

The End of the Month Review process can be adapted to many different learners.  Begin by supporting your learner to evaluate the month with help in each area.  Then continue to fade support until your learner can evaluate the strengths and weaknesses of the previous month independently.  Mastery criteria for this exercise include 3 consecutive months of evaluating progress and adapting the next month's activities accordingly.

#### Expansion Ideas: End of Month Review Can Be Combined with Many Different Exercises in the Book. for Example, Use It As Part of the Weekly Planning Meeting Agenda (see Exercise #3) and the Weekly Binder (see Exercise #31). in Addition, If Needed, Use the End of Month Review As an Opportunity to Practice Other Exercises in Other Executive Functioning Areas.
